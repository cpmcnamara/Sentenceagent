# Hiring Boundary-Thinkers

<!-- SPINE: The most valuable consultants in AI aren't the most credentialed or even the most curious—they're boundary-thinkers, and most consulting systems accidentally filter them out. -->

## The Warning Label

The feedback on his interview read like a warning label: "doesn't communicate well," "slide deck skills below par," "uncomfortable in client settings." The firm's evaluation system had him ranked lowest in his class. By every metric that consulting firms use to sort candidates, he was failing before he'd started.

But I'd seen something in how he approached problems. He didn't decorate his thinking with frameworks or jargon. When given a messy scenario, he went straight to what mattered—even when he couldn't explain why it mattered in language that satisfied his reviewers. So I pulled him onto my program and gave him a challenge that I've since given to many others, though few have passed it: stop building decks just to build decks. Figure out what you actually need to communicate and do it in one or two slides. Find the evidence, the numbers, the core insight that would make those slides sufficient.

Most consultants fail this challenge completely. They cannot escape the gravitational pull of the twenty-slide deck, the generic jargon, the polished emptiness. This consultant found his way through. And watching him do it, I began to understand why he'd struggled so badly in evaluations: he was a boundary-thinker trapped in a system that rewarded boundary-ignorers.

Today he's one of our strongest people. Not because he became more like everyone else, but because he learned to express his difference in ways clients could use.


## The Mistake I Made

Before I understood boundary-thinking, I made a different bet. My background is in chemical engineering, and I assumed that domain knowledge was the key—that someone who understood the industry would naturally bridge the gap between what AI could do and what clients needed.

I hired two people specifically for their energy industry experience. Both struggled. Not with the domain itself—they knew the terminology, they understood the physics—but with the actual work of deploying AI solutions. They couldn't translate between worlds the way I'd assumed they would. Meanwhile, our core consultants, the ones without special domain backgrounds, were far more effective. They learned what they needed by working closely with client stakeholders, and they didn't carry the baggage of assuming they already understood.

Domain knowledge matters less than I thought. Curiosity matters more than I expected. But neither is the thing that actually predicts who will be valuable. The trait that matters most isn't domain-specific or even AI-specific. I call it boundary-thinking.


## What Boundary-Thinking Actually Is

Boundary-thinking has three parts.

**First: seeing edges.** A boundary-thinker looks at a problem and immediately sees where its edges actually are. Most consultants frame problems according to what they've done before, barely remembering how they came to do things that way in the first place. Boundary-thinkers can't work that way even if they try. Their mind never sees the same problem as the same problem. They reconstruct it fresh every time.

**Second: finding bottlenecks.** Consider how you'd optimize flow through an airport security line. The naive approach looks at the length of the line—more people waiting means slower, so speed everything up. The experienced approach finds the bottleneck. Maybe the bag scanner. Maybe the body scanner. Maybe the ID check. Understanding the system means understanding these bottlenecks: what's embodied in them, why they exist, where the next one will form once you've addressed the first. Most AI consulting treats every constraint as a problem to be automated away. Boundary-thinkers treat constraints as structure to be understood.

**Third: placing effort where it changes systems.** Many consultants will build an intent list just to have one—another deliverable for the deck. But a boundary-thinker sees that every intent is an atomic organizing principle, that the intents don't just describe customer behavior but structure everything downstream. Get the intent architecture wrong and nothing else compensates. The boundary-thinker sees the atoms forming the organism, and once you see it that way, the path to building the whole thing becomes visible.

Curiosity is necessary for this work but insufficient. A curious consultant gets excited about how intents work, reads up on approaches, experiments with models. A boundary-thinking consultant sees why the intents matter more than the models—and spends time accordingly.


## Five Things Boundary-Thinkers Do Differently

When you work with a boundary-thinker long enough, you notice patterns. Here's what distinguishes them from people who are merely smart or curious:

**1. They ask about constraints before solutions.** Instead of jumping to "how do we build this," they ask "what's actually limiting success here?" They treat constraints as the specification rather than the obstacle.

**2. They produce less polished work that's more useful.** Their slides might have rough edges, but they contain the core insight. They've escaped the gravitational pull of the twenty-slide deck full of jargon that says nothing distinctive.

**3. They can explain what they don't know.** Boundary-thinkers have a mental map of the problem, and they can point to the blank spaces. They say "we need to figure out X before we can move on Y" rather than glossing over uncertainty.

**4. They resist adding complexity.** When something seems too complicated, they assume the framing is wrong rather than the solution being incomplete. They simplify by redefining the problem.

**5. They change their approach when the edges move.** New information doesn't just add to their understanding—it restructures it. They rebuild their mental model rather than patching it.

The uncomfortable truth: most consulting evaluation systems inadvertently select against these behaviors. We reward polish over insight, confidence over accuracy, coverage over depth. The boundary-thinkers who survive are the ones lucky enough to find a mentor who recognizes what they're doing.


## Interview Exercises That Reveal Boundary-Thinking

Generic interview questions don't surface boundary-thinking. "Tell me about a time you solved a hard problem" invites rehearsed stories that reveal nothing. You need exercises that force candidates to think in front of you.

**Exercise 1: The Bottleneck Problem**

Give the candidate a simple system with obvious inefficiency—a slow approval process, a backed-up pipeline, a service that's timing out. Ask them to diagnose it.

*Pass:* They ask clarifying questions about where the slowdown occurs. They identify that fixing one bottleneck might create another. They think about the system rather than the symptom.

*Fail:* They jump immediately to a solution. They propose a technology without understanding the constraint. They treat the problem as isolated rather than embedded in a larger flow.

**Exercise 2: The Two-Slide Deck**

Give the candidate a complex business scenario with lots of data. Ask them to create a two-slide presentation that captures what matters. Time limit: thirty minutes.

*Pass:* They identify the core question and answer it. The slides have an argument, not just a summary. They made choices about what to leave out.

*Fail:* They try to fit everything in, shrinking fonts and cramming information. The slides describe the situation but make no claim. They ask for more slides rather than making harder choices.

Neither exercise is about getting the "right" answer. Boundary-thinkers reveal themselves through how they engage with the problem—the questions they ask, the parts they focus on, the complexity they shed rather than add.


## A Scoring Rubric

If you want to make boundary-thinking part of your evaluation process, you need to be specific about what you're looking for. Here's a simple rubric:

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **1** | Jumps to solutions without understanding the problem. Adds complexity when stuck. Cannot articulate what they don't know. |
| **2** | Asks some clarifying questions but gets pulled toward familiar patterns. Produces work that covers the space without making claims. |
| **3** | Identifies key constraints. Can distinguish between important and unimportant parts of the problem. Work has a point of view. |
| **4** | Restructures problems when the standard framing doesn't fit. Produces unusually concise work that captures what matters. Explains uncertainty clearly. |
| **5** | Sees edges others miss. Finds the governing bottleneck quickly. Produces work so focused it makes others' efforts look bloated. Changes their model when new information arrives. |

Most candidates score 2-3. A score of 4 is rare and worth hiring for even when other skills are unpolished. A score of 5 is someone you build around.


## Building a Team That Develops Boundary-Thinkers

Hiring boundary-thinkers is only half the problem. You also need a system that develops them—and doesn't accidentally filter them out after they arrive.

The consultant I opened with needed something specific: he had the ability to see problems clearly but lacked the ability to communicate that clarity in client settings. The question wasn't whether he was good enough. The question was whether we would let the evaluation system spit him out or treat him as an apprentice and help him develop what he lacked.

We made the bet. He learned to translate his boundary-thinking into language clients could follow. He didn't become a normal consultant—he became something better, because the core skill was already there.

This requires a few things from the team:

**A strong project lead who protects development.** Someone has to notice when a boundary-thinker is struggling with polish rather than substance, and create space for them to grow the weaker skill without abandoning the stronger one.

**Clear accountabilities that reward insight over coverage.** If the incentive is to produce more slides, you'll get more slides. If the incentive is to answer the right question, boundary-thinkers will thrive.

**A norm that asking for help is normal.** Boundary-thinkers often get stuck when their clarity of thought outruns their ability to execute. They need support that doesn't make them feel like failures.

**An explicit acknowledgment that the system is still evolving.** The best people will help you build the system as they work within it, but only if they believe it's malleable.


## The Uncomfortable Truth

Here's what I didn't expect when I started building this team: the people who matter most almost don't arrive at all. Their interview feedback is a warning label. The evaluation system ranks them lowest. They're awkward in settings where polish is expected. If you're not paying attention—if you're just running the standard process—they get filtered out and you never know what you lost.

The teams that win in AI consulting aren't the ones with the most credentials or the most domain experience. They're the ones that recognize boundary-thinking as the rare skill it is—and build systems that can find it, develop it, and use it.

Most of us don't know how to hire for what we actually need. We're all still learning what the new shape of consulting looks like. But the starting point is admitting that our standard processes select against the very people who see problems most clearly.

Boundary-thinkers don't look like consultants. That's the point.
