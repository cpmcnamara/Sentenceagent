# Boundary-Thinkers

He came through our hiring program with feedback that read like a warning label: "doesn't communicate well," "slide deck skills below par," "uncomfortable in client settings." The firm's evaluation system had him ranked lowest in his class. By the conventional metrics of consulting—the polish, the presence, the ability to fill a room with confidence—he was failing before he'd started.

But I'd seen something in how he approached problems. He didn't decorate his thinking with frameworks or jargon. He went straight to what mattered, even when he couldn't explain why it mattered in language that satisfied his reviewers. So I pulled him onto my program and gave him a challenge: stop building decks just to build decks. Figure out what you actually need to communicate and find a way to do it in one or two slides. Find the evidence, the numbers, the core insight that would make those slides sufficient.

Many consultants fail this challenge completely. They cannot escape the gravitational pull of the twenty-slide deck, the one full of generic jargon that says nothing distinctive. But this consultant found his way through—and watching him do it, I began to understand why he'd struggled so badly in standard evaluations. He was a boundary-thinker trapped in a system that rewarded boundary-ignorers.

---

Most consultants frame problems according to what they've done before, barely remembering how they came to do things that way in the first place. They see a contact center engagement and reach for the contact center playbook. They see an energy client and apply the energy template. This isn't laziness—it feels like efficiency. But it means they never actually see the problem in front of them.

Boundary-thinkers can't work this way. Their minds won't let them. They look at a contact center AI strategy and see that intent detection—understanding what customers are trying to accomplish—isn't just one component among many. It's the atomic organizing principle. Get the intent architecture wrong and nothing downstream compensates. The boundary-thinker sees the system as a system, and once you see it that way, the path to building it becomes visible.

If you want to understand what boundary-thinking looks like, consider how you'd optimize flow through an airport security line.

The naive approach looks at the length of the line—more people waiting means slower, so speed everything up. The experienced approach finds the bottleneck. Maybe the bag scanner. Maybe the body scanner. Maybe the ID check. Understanding the problem requires understanding which constraint actually governs the system. Once you debottleneck one, you ask: where will the next one form?

But here's what most people miss: the bottlenecks aren't problems to be eliminated. They're functions to be understood. There's knowledge embedded in each one—rules, decisions, requirements, each aligned to a specific constraint. The security line exists for reasons.

This is exactly what most AI consulting gets wrong. They treat every constraint as a problem to be automated away rather than a function to be understood. Either they ignore the bottleneck and hope for the best, or they try to replace it without understanding why it exists. What they rarely do is ask: what knowledge is embedded here? How do we make that knowledge more usable—without pretending the constraint doesn't serve a purpose?

---

One of our stakeholders said what everyone was thinking: "I don't want to hear one more firm tell me RAG will solve this mess."

He was right. The client had 200,000 documents in their commercial operations—accumulated over decades, touched by thousands of people who had long since moved on. Every other firm had proposed the same thing: just connect a language model to your documents and let it answer questions.

The problem wasn't retrieval. The problem was that no one had done the work to understand what was actually in those documents, how they connected to what people needed to do, or how to tell the difference between knowledge that mattered and knowledge that was just taking up space.

That's boundary-thinking applied to a real problem. Not "how do we deploy the technology?" but "what's actually constraining this system, and how do we work with it rather than around it?"

---

I used to believe something different. My background is in chemical engineering, and I assumed domain knowledge was the key. If someone understood the industry—if they'd worked in energy or manufacturing—they'd be able to bridge the gap between what AI could do and what the client needed.

I hired two people specifically for their energy sector experience. Both struggled. Not with the domain—they knew the terminology, they understood the physics—but with the ability to see problems fresh. They couldn't translate between worlds the way I'd assumed they would. Meanwhile, our generalists, the ones without special domain backgrounds, were far more effective. They learned what they needed by working with stakeholders, and they didn't carry the baggage of assuming they already understood.

Domain knowledge creates blindness as often as it creates insight. The expert sees what they expect to see. The boundary-thinker sees what's actually there.

---

The consultant I opened with—the one ranked lowest in his class—is now one of our strongest people. Not because he became more like everyone else, but because he learned to express his difference in ways clients could use.

The question we faced with him wasn't whether he was good enough. The question was whether we would let the system spit him out or treat him as an apprentice. He had a genuinely different way of seeing, but he needed the complementary skills: the ability to deliver in client settings, to communicate clearly, to build decks that were sparse AND complete.

That required a project lead who could protect his development while he built what he lacked. Clear accountabilities—not just "improve your presentations" but specific gaps with specific plans. A norm that asking for help on the polish stuff was normal, because his value wasn't in the polish.

It took time. By the end, he could hold a room, explain complex systems in language executives understood, build the kind of work product that satisfied clients without sacrificing depth. He hadn't become a different person. He had developed around his core difference rather than in spite of it.

---

Here's what I still get wrong: I believe I can spot boundary-thinkers intuitively, and I probably can't.

I've made hires that felt right and weren't. I've passed on people who might have been exceptional. Humans are too complex to evaluate on vibes, and yet vibes are how most of us make hiring decisions.

The research is clear: structured approaches outperform unstructured ones. So we've started making the intuition concrete. Not "I have a feeling about this candidate" but "I notice they asked about constraints before solutions." Not "they seem smart" but "their analysis identified the governing bottleneck within ten minutes while others never found it."

The structured observation doesn't replace judgment. It gives judgment something to push against.

---

The uncomfortable truth is that most consulting systems are designed to filter out the people they most need.

The polished candidates score well on standard assessments. The ones who might be exceptional score badly on standard assessments—and well on assessments that don't exist yet. The people who built the evaluation process optimized for people like themselves, because who else would they know how to recognize?

So the pipeline fills with people who are very good at seeming like consultants, while the genuine outliers get filtered out somewhere between the resume screen and the case interview.

The consultant I opened with made it through by accident. A fluke of timing, a manager with a hunch, a willingness to override the system. That's not scalable. But it's where we start: with the admission that we don't actually know how to find the people we most need, and with the commitment to build a system that gives them room to develop rather than expecting them to arrive fully formed.

The most valuable consultants aren't the most credentialed, or even the most curious. They're the ones who see problems differently—who reconstruct every situation fresh, who gravitate toward the parts that actually determine outcomes.

Most systems filter them out. That's the problem I'm still learning to solve.
