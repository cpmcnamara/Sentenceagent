# The Four-Eyed Monster

## SITUATION_SETUP

Consulting is a four-eyed monster. The first eye watches client projects—the work that pays the bills and proves you can deliver. The second eye watches cohesive assets—the frameworks, tools, and intellectual property that make you more than a collection of freelancers. The third eye watches collective knowledge sharing—the conversations, lessons, and institutional memory that compound over time. The fourth eye watches diversity of skills—the range of capabilities that lets you attack problems from angles your competitors can't see.

When the monster is healthy, these four eyes merge into one. You stop noticing which eye is doing the seeing. The team just works. But building that team—getting the eyes to align in the first place—requires understanding that each eye has its own demands, its own failure modes, its own way of going blind.

We didn't set out to build an AI practice the way most firms have. In fact, we resisted as long as possible. I wouldn't call us AI skeptics, but our orientation was always toward the problems of our clients rather than the imagined problems that emerged when the AI boom first hit. We thought constantly about limitations and strengths, about what AI could actually do in an enterprise versus what it was being sold to do.

The turning point came during a knowledge management engagement. The client had over 200,000 documents in their commercial operations—a complete mess, accumulated over decades, touched by thousands of people who had long since moved on. They wanted to use AI to make sense of it. Every other firm they'd talked to had proposed some version of retrieval-augmented generation: just connect a language model to your documents and let it answer questions.

One of our stakeholders finally said what everyone was thinking: "I don't want to hear one more firm tell me RAG will solve this mess."

He was right. The problem wasn't retrieval. The problem was that no one had done the work to understand what was actually in those documents, how they connected to what people needed to do, or how to tell the difference between knowledge that mattered and knowledge that was just taking up space. So we built something different: an evaluation framework that could systematically identify what users were actually trying to accomplish, align documentation to those intents, suggest how documents should be curated and restructured, and measure whether any of it was working. It was an orchestrated approach to getting the seventy-five percent of know-how that lives in people's heads into a system that could actually use it.

That project became the backbone of everything we've done since. We've expanded into contact centers, customer-facing applications, operational systems—but the core insight remains the same. You have to identify the limits of AI and build around them, evaluate what's working in a way that's seamless and efficient, and never lose sight of the fact that the enterprise already has knowledge embedded everywhere. The job isn't to replace that knowledge with something artificial. The job is to make the knowledge usable.

I don't think we're unique in what we're doing. What's different is how we orchestrate it, and our ruthless focus on making systems work rather than making demos that impress.

## EXPECTED_ADVICE_REJECTED

The standard advice for building a consulting team is to hire people who look like consultants. Polish and presence, the ability to fill a room with confidence even when the ideas are thin. For AI teams specifically, the advice mutates: hire AI experts, get the credentials, poach the data scientists with publication records and the engineers with impressive logos on their resumes.

I used to believe this, and I've been wrong in ways that taught me something important.

One area where I over-indexed was domain experience. My background is in chemical engineering, and I think that made me arrogant about how much domain knowledge matters. I assumed that if someone understood the industry—if they'd worked in energy or manufacturing or whatever vertical we were serving—they'd be able to bridge the gap between what AI could do and what the client needed.

I hired two people specifically because of their energy industry experience. Both struggled. Not with the domain itself—they knew the terminology, they understood the physics—but with the nuances of how we approached AI problems and the ability to actually deploy solutions in their respective domains. They couldn't translate between the worlds the way I'd assumed they would. Meanwhile, our core consultants, the ones without any special domain background, were far more effective. They learned what they needed to learn by working closely with client stakeholders, and they didn't carry the baggage of assuming they already understood.

What I've come to believe is that domain knowledge matters far less than the ability to work with stakeholders from the domain in a way that makes them want to support the mission. The consultant's job isn't to be the expert—it's to make the experts feel heard and to channel what they know into something that works. Domain experience as a shortcut to credibility can help, but only if it positions you to listen rather than to lecture.

And yet: I can't pretend domain experience doesn't matter at all. The backbone of this team, and my ability to start hiring in the first place, came from helping a chemical client develop AI use cases—something I could only do because that's where I started my career. This is the hard part of the four-eyed monster. There are so many things that don't work, aren't ideal, aren't perfect, and yet you still can't live without them in some form. The skill isn't finding the right answer. The skill is holding multiple partial answers together and building something that works anyway.

## REFRAMING_PREMISE

Where we've gotten better at hiring is in making gut feelings concrete—turning hunches into criteria you can point to and test against.

One criterion has not led us astray: hiring the raw curious, the people who will get up early or stay late for no reason other than to learn. Not because anyone told them to. Not because some rule demanded it. Just because they had to know.

The ability to learn while building is the trait that compounds fastest. People who have it can start off lost, visibly confused, clearly behind. Then they get a little better, then a little better, until they start to look competent, then strong, then they become the kind of person you could never have hired from outside—because by the time someone looks that good, they've already been claimed. The only way to get them is to grow them, and the only way to grow them is to bet on learning potential over current knowledge.

But I've realized something that surprised me. Curiosity is necessary and insufficient. The rarer skill, the one that isn't technical or AI-specific at all, is what I've come to call boundary-thinking: the ability to look at a problem, see where its edges actually are, and spend your time on the parts that matter rather than the parts that merely exist.

Most consultants frame problems according to what they've done before, barely remembering how they came to do things that way in the first place. Curiosity alone doesn't fix this; it just makes them more enthusiastic about repeating familiar patterns. The hiring practice that never fails is finding people who can't think that way even if they tried. They can't, because their mind never sees the same problem as the same problem. They reconstruct it fresh every time, and they naturally gravitate toward the parts that will actually determine the outcome.

## MECHANISM_REVEALED

If you want to understand boundary-thinking, consider how you'd optimize flow through an airport security line.

The naive approach is to look at the length of the line—more people waiting means a slower process, so you try to speed everything up. But the experienced approach is to find the bottleneck and understand what's actually happening there. Maybe it's the bag scanning machine. Maybe it's the body scanner. Maybe it's the ID check at the front. Understanding the problem requires understanding the nature of these bottlenecks—if you don't understand them, you don't understand the system at all. And once you debottleneck one, you have to ask: where will the next one form?

It's not enough to know that a bottleneck exists. You need to understand what's embodied in that bottleneck and how to work with it. There's knowledge, steps, rules, decisions, requirements—each aligned to a specific constraint. The security line exists for reasons. The bottlenecks aren't problems to be eliminated; they're functions to be understood.

This is exactly what most AI consulting gets wrong in the enterprise. They treat every constraint as a problem to be automated away rather than a function to be understood and supported. Either they ignore the bottleneck and hope for the best, or they try to replace it without understanding why it exists. What they rarely do is ask: what knowledge is embedded in this constraint? How can we make that knowledge more accessible, more consistent, more usable—without pretending the constraint doesn't serve a purpose?

Let me tell you about a consultant who understood this instinctively.

He came through our hiring program with feedback that read like a warning label: "doesn't communicate well," "slide deck skills below par," "uncomfortable in client settings." The firm's evaluation system had him ranked lowest in his class. By the conventional metrics of consulting—the first eye, the client-facing polish—he was failing before he'd started.

But I'd seen something in how he approached problems. He didn't decorate his thinking with frameworks or jargon. He went straight to what mattered, even when he couldn't explain why it mattered in language that satisfied his reviewers. So I pulled him onto my program and gave him a challenge: stop building decks just to build decks. Figure out what you actually need to communicate and find a way to do it in one or two slides. Find the evidence, the numbers, the core insight that would make those slides sufficient. Do that every time, no matter how much pressure you feel to pad it out.

Many people fail this challenge completely. They cannot escape the gravitational pull of the twenty-slide deck, the one full of generic jargon that says nothing distinctive. But this consultant found his way through—and watching him do it, I began to understand why he'd struggled so badly in standard evaluations. He was a boundary-thinker trapped in a system that rewarded boundary-ignorers. He naturally saw problems in terms of their structure, their constraints, their essential moving parts. But the firm's training had told him that "good" looked like outputs that were polished but empty.

The question we faced wasn't whether he was good enough. The question was whether we would let the system spit him out or treat him as an apprentice and help him develop what he lacked. He had the fourth eye—a genuinely different way of seeing—but he needed the first eye, the ability to deliver in client settings without losing the depth of his thinking.

That bet paid off. Today he's one of our strongest people, not because he became more like everyone else, but because he learned to express his difference in ways that clients could use. The four-eyed monster needs people like him—people who see what others miss—but it also needs a system that doesn't reject them before they've had a chance to grow their other capabilities.

## CONTRAST_SERIES

**Curiosity versus boundary-thinking.**

At the beginning of 2024, I thought the future consultant was simply a curious one—the kind who tries things because they have to, not because anyone told them to. I still believe curiosity matters enormously. Consulting has long attracted people who drink the firm Kool-Aid, who feel like they know more than their clients about some mysterious strategy concept but couldn't articulate what exactly they know. That kind of consultant is dying. The future belongs to people who learn faster than anyone else.

But curiosity is secondary to something harder to find.

Consider a contact center AI strategy, where a large portion of the work involves intent detection—understanding what customers are trying to accomplish when they call or message. A curious consultant gets excited about how intents work, reads up on the latest approaches, experiments with different models. But a boundary-thinking consultant sees something deeper: that every intent is an atomic organizing principle, that the intents don't just describe customer behavior but structure everything downstream. If you get the intent architecture wrong, nothing else you build will compensate. The boundary-thinker sees the problem as a system of atoms forming an organism, and once you see it that way, the path to building the whole thing becomes visible.

Most consultants will build an intent list just to have one—another deliverable to add to the deck. Curiosity makes them more enthusiastic about this work, but it doesn't stop them from losing sight of why the work matters.

**Constraints versus problems.**

This connects to a deeper confusion I see everywhere in AI consulting: treating constraints as problems rather than as structure.

Think back to the airport security line. Each bottleneck serves a purpose—checking IDs, scanning bags, screening people. You might argue some of these steps shouldn't exist, but that's a different argument. Within the system as it exists, each constraint embodies knowledge about what matters and what can go wrong. The right approach isn't to eliminate the constraint but to understand what's embedded in it and make that knowledge more effective.

Most AI consulting in the enterprise does one of two things: either it ignores the constraints and hopes for the best, or it tries to replace them without understanding why they exist. What it rarely does is ask how to make the knowledge inside the constraint more accessible, more consistent, more usable. When you take constraints seriously—when you treat them as the specification rather than the obstacle—strategy takes on a different shape. You stop producing the same generic deck everyone has seen a million times and start building something that corresponds to reality.

**Boring versus shiny.**

You might think that everything I'm describing makes consulting sound tedious. But there's only one thing that's truly boring in this work: building something impressive that nobody uses.

My early AI work was in chemical plants and refineries—developing predictive monitoring models using neural networks, statistical methods, and rule-based systems. Large programs doing genuinely novel things. But what we lacked, for years, was the boring part: embedding these models into interfaces simple enough that operators could use them without thinking about "AI" at all.

It took sitting with a brilliant operator-turned-engineer to understand what we'd been missing. After years of poor adoption, we built a new interface—stripped down, almost stupidly simple—and put it in the control room. Operators started using it overnight. No elaborate change management campaign, no training programs. Just something that fit into how they already worked. The complex model underneath was exactly the same. What changed was the boring part: how it showed up in their day.

**Learning versus knowing.**

The irony of hiring for learning potential in AI is that the best learners often don't seem the most up-to-date. They're not dabbling with every new tool or breathlessly tracking every announcement. They've found a core set of tools they trust, and they've gone deep—learning not just how to use them but when they're the wrong choice.

Our strongest consultants are the ones who can explain what they know to others, not because we require it, but because the act of explaining forces a clarity that nothing else provides. People who teach demonstrate learning more powerfully than people who merely consume, and they get more from the teaching than the students do.

The firms that hire for credentials are betting on what people already know. We've learned to bet on how fast people can come to know what they need to—and whether they can bring others along once they do.

## ACTIONABLE_ADVICE

We hear endless talk about the collapsing consulting pyramid—how junior people are becoming less relevant while certain seniors become more productive, how the layers in between may be the most at risk. I find most of this analysis shallow. Consulting has always been a people-centric business, and the pyramid exists because developing people takes time and structure. The question isn't whether the pyramid will survive, but how its shape will change as the work itself changes.

What's undeniable is that AI is forcing roles to shift. Data scientists are becoming AI systems engineers, but not every experienced data scientist can make that transition, and not every new hire has the support they need to develop in this direction. Different people need different scaffolding. The idea that you can drop someone onto a project and expect them to figure everything out—that apprenticeship is obsolete—misunderstands what makes consulting teams work.

So how do you rethink the pyramid while everything else is in flux?

What I want to emphasize is that a unifying system of work is not technically complex. It's the scaffolding that lets people with different skills and different levels of experience work together without silos and actually grow together. For us, it comes down to a few simple elements.

First, you need a strong project lead who pulls everyone together on every program, and a team that trusts this person will step in when things get messy. Second, you need painfully clear accountabilities. On hard projects, most breakdowns happen because people don't actually know what they're supposed to deliver, or at what level of detail. You need a map of who owns which part of the work and how those parts connect. Third, you need ad hoc support wired in so no one feels like they're on an island. People should know exactly who to go to when they're stuck, and asking for help should be normal rather than a sign of weakness. Finally, you have to say out loud that this system is not fixed. It exists to evolve, and everyone working inside it is expected to help evolve it.

In that sense, the future consulting project looks less like a series of isolated engagements and more like an ongoing shared codebase—except the "code" is how we decompose problems, how we share knowledge, how we make decisions together. Every consulting team needs its own version of this: an open-ended project with clear leadership and governance, real accountability, and a genuine sense of mission behind it. The vine that connects the branches.

What makes a functioning consulting firm remarkable is that you can't replicate it by studying its assets or org chart. What works is embedded in the system itself—the fine-tuned processes, yes, but also the spirit, the shared conviction that we're building something no one else has done for a client. The monster's four eyes have to believe they're looking at the same thing.

## OBJECTION_ANTICIPATED

The nature of any consulting firm is a hybrid focus on sales and delivery. When we first articulated our approach—the emphasis on understanding constraints, on orchestrating knowledge rather than just deploying models—there was little interest from leadership. Part of the problem was language: when we talked about "context engineering," people heard it as rebranded "prompt engineering" and dismissed it accordingly.

This taught us something about how new ideas spread inside firms. You have to solve two problems at once: building the actual capability, and finding words that make the capability legible to people who haven't thought about it as deeply as you have. The translation problem isn't separate from the work—it is part of the work. And when you solve it, something interesting happens: explaining becomes exciting. Every skeptical question becomes an invitation to go deeper, and that enthusiasm is visible. People can sense when you've thought about something more than they have, and most will defer rather than challenge at a level they're not prepared for.

But the harder objection I've faced is about my own limitations. I used to think I had some intuitive gift for evaluating candidates—a sense for who would fit, who would grow, who would surprise us. I've come to believe this is largely self-deception. Humans are too complex to evaluate on vibes, and yet vibes are how most of us make hiring decisions.

The research is clear: structured interviews substantially outperform unstructured ones. My problem is that I think in systems, so I'm drawn to people who add something new to the system even when they have obvious gaps. But consulting teams aren't purely mechanical systems where each part serves a specialized function. They're social-professional systems, and there's always a thick core layer of skills—communication, client presence, basic reliability—that people have to master before their unique strengths become valuable.

This is why we're moving toward more structured approaches, even as we try to preserve room for the outliers who don't fit standard patterns. And it's why diversity matters so much more than most firms acknowledge. The research shows that well-managed diverse teams consider more alternatives and make better decisions. But poorly managed diverse teams suffer from misunderstandings and slow consensus. The question isn't whether diversity helps—it's whether you've built a system that can actually use it.

Over time, I hope we can lower the threshold of the core layer and move toward something more like a rainforest: a rich ecosystem of specialists connected by shared infrastructure rather than uniform training. But that requires team design far beyond what most firms have achieved, including us.

## CLOSE_WITH_GIFT

What still surprises me about AI consulting is that it's the most human form of consulting there is.

No subject touches as much fear, confusion, excitement, and linguistic chaos as AI does right now. And no form of consulting requires such careful work to embed human knowledge into systems—a task that resembles anthropology more than engineering, that forces you to understand how people actually think and decide and know. When it's done well, no other consulting creates value as immediately visible to clients. They can see their own knowledge coming back to them, structured and extended in ways they couldn't have done alone.

Ben Horowitz wrote about something he called The Struggle: "The Struggle is when you wonder why you started the company in the first place. The Struggle is when people ask you why you don't quit and you don't know the answer. The Struggle is when your employees think you are lying and you think they may be right. The Struggle is when food loses its taste... The Struggle is not failure, but it causes failure. Especially if you are weak."

Building an AI consulting team is The Struggle. It's wondering why you're doing this at all. It's watching the four-eyed monster refuse to cohere. It's betting on people who stumble and having to decide whether to help them through or let them go.

But here's what I didn't expect: the struggle is where you find the people who matter. Not because suffering is noble, but because the struggle reveals who can see problems fresh, who understands constraints rather than just complaining about them, who does the boring work that makes the complex work function.

You don't find those people by looking for consultants who look like consultants. You find them by building a system with room for apprenticeship, with patience for translation, with belief that the four eyes can learn to see as one. You find them by accepting that some of what you need won't work perfectly—that you'll hire for domain experience and be wrong, that you'll trust your intuition and miss—and building something that works anyway.

That's the uncomfortable truth about AI consulting: none of us know how to hire for what we actually need. We're all still learning what the new shape of consulting looks like. The teams that will win are the ones honest enough to admit this—and structured enough to keep getting better anyway.
