# The Four-Eyed Monster

## SITUATION_SETUP

Consulting is a four-eyed monster. The first eye watches client projects—the work that pays the bills and proves you can deliver. The second eye watches cohesive assets—the frameworks, tools, and intellectual property that make you more than a collection of freelancers. The third eye watches collective knowledge sharing—the conversations, the lessons passed between teams, the institutional memory that compounds over time. The fourth eye watches diversity of skills—the range of capabilities that lets you attack problems from angles your competitors can't see.

When the monster is healthy, these four eyes merge into one. You stop noticing which eye is doing the seeing. The team just works. But building that team—getting the eyes to align in the first place—requires understanding that each eye has its own demands, its own failure modes, its own way of going blind.

Paul Graham writes that startups take off because founders make them take off. There may be a handful that grew by themselves, but usually it takes a push—like the crank that car engines had before electric starters. Once the engine was going, it would keep going, but there was a separate and laborious process to get it going. Building a consulting team works the same way. Ours didn't magically appear. We didn't start as an "AI team" or even a "data team." We were solving problems right in front of us—optimizing data centers, supporting nuclear shipyards, improving chemical plants—and along the way our people were earning patents on work they'd never done before.

But what we lacked was the thing that makes a group into a team: the alignment of those four eyes into a single way of seeing. And this, I've come to believe, is the core challenge of all consulting. AI teams are no different, except they raise a question that other practices can avoid: why build one at all? What's the missing problem that justifies the existence of yet another AI consultancy?

Anyone who's been a consultant—a real one—knows the work is about helping clients solve something hard and watching a team come together in a focused, strange, only-in-consulting way to get it done. Early AI teams were either software development shops or data science shops. But where was the team that could truly break down how AI fits into an enterprise? That could dissect all the fine-tuned business physics—the rules, the domain know-how, everything needed to make a decision that a human would trust? That's the team I wanted to build. That's the four-eyed monster I'm still learning to feed.

## EXPECTED_ADVICE_REJECTED

The standard advice for building a consulting team is to hire people who look like consultants. Polish and presence, the ability to fill a room with confidence even when the ideas are thin. For AI teams specifically, the advice mutates: hire AI experts, get the credentials, poach the data scientists with publication records and the engineers with FAANG on their resumes.

I used to believe this. I was far too optimistic about people with the right backgrounds—my inclination was to look for what they did well and assume the rest would follow. It's a bit like dating someone whose flaws you think you can fix: if there's some intuitive spark, some hunch that this could work, surely we can make it work.

What I've learned is that my intuitive hiring guesses have been horribly wrong—and also wonderfully right. After all my experience, I'm not sure I'd change much, because the strength of the rights far outweighs the pain of the wrongs. But I would change what I'm looking for.

I see it constantly in consulting: people hiring people who feel just like them, same background, same ways of working, same corporate blandness. I'd rather struggle with all the gambles for the few who break this mold. One of the great things about leading a consulting team is getting to see, again and again, how little you actually know. I am a horrible manager of project details, yet our team would crumble without the people who are good at that—they keep the first eye open, the one watching client delivery. My client skills are strong in understanding problems but unremarkable in simply being someone clients love—no firm survives without those people either, the ones who keep relationships warm even when there's nothing to sell.

What I want to write about is the scaffolding that's less discussed: the specific ways AI consulting forces you to rethink which of the four eyes you're developing in each person. And maybe the least-discussed truth of all is that good AI consulting isn't fundamentally different from classical consulting done well. The monster has always had four eyes. AI just makes it harder to pretend otherwise.

## REFRAMING_PREMISE

Where we've gotten better at hiring is in making gut feelings concrete—turning hunches into criteria you can actually point to.

One criterion has not led us astray in the AI age: hiring the raw curious, the people who will get up early or stay late for no reason other than to learn. Not because anyone told them to. Not because some rule demanded it. Just because they had to know.

The ability to learn while building is the trait that compounds fastest on our team. People who have it can start off lost, visibly confused, clearly behind. Then they get a little better, then a little better, until they start to look competent, then strong, then they become the unicorn you could never have hired from outside—because by the time someone looks like a unicorn, they've already been claimed by someone else. The only way to get them is to grow them. And the only way to grow them is to bet on learning potential over current knowledge.

But I've realized something that surprised me. Curiosity is a secondary skill to something even rarer, something that isn't technical or AI-specific at all.

I call it boundary-thinking: the ability to look at a problem, see where its edges actually are, and spend your time on the parts that matter rather than the parts that merely exist. It's an almost mathematical instinct for structure—being able to look at an AI system and understand immediately which components carry the weight, which constraints will determine success, and which impressive-sounding features are irrelevant decoration.

Most consultants frame problems according to what they've done before, barely remembering how they came to do things that way in the first place. Curiosity alone doesn't fix this; it just makes them more enthusiastic about repeating familiar patterns. The hiring practice that never fails is finding people who can't think that way even if they tried. They can't, because their mind never sees the same problem as the same problem. They reconstruct it fresh every time, and they naturally gravitate toward the parts that will actually determine the outcome.

## MECHANISM_REVEALED

Let me tell you about a consultant I almost lost.

He came through our hiring program with feedback that read like a warning label: "doesn't communicate well," "slide deck skills are below par," "seems uncomfortable in client settings." The firm's evaluation system had him ranked lowest in his class. By the conventional metrics of consulting—the first eye, the client-facing polish—he was failing.

But I'd seen something in how he thought about problems. He didn't decorate his thinking with jargon or impressive-sounding frameworks. He went straight to what mattered, even when he couldn't explain why it mattered in language that satisfied his reviewers. So I pulled him onto my program and gave him a challenge that I've since given to many others, though few have passed it: stop building decks just to build decks. Instead, figure out what you actually need to communicate and find a way to do it in one or two slides. Find the evidence, the numbers, the core insight that would make those slides sufficient. Do that every time, no matter how much pressure you feel to pad it out.

Many consultants fail this challenge completely. They cannot escape the gravitational pull of the twenty-slide deck full of generic jargon that says nothing distinctive. But this consultant found his way through. And watching him do it, I began to understand why he'd struggled so badly in the standard evaluation: he was a boundary-thinker trapped in a system that rewarded boundary-ignorers. He naturally saw problems in terms of their structure, their constraints, their essential moving parts—but the firm's training had told him that "good" looked like outputs that were polished but empty. Problem-thinkers find meaning in brevity, in thought, in the architecture of an argument. They're rare. And they're rarer still because our processes weed them out. We kill the unicorns that are right in front of us because we expect them to arrive with their horns already showing.

The question we faced with this consultant wasn't whether he was good enough. The question was whether we would let the system spit him out or treat him as an apprentice and help him develop what he lacked. He had the fourth eye—a genuinely different way of seeing—but he needed the first eye, the ability to deliver in client settings without losing the depth of his thinking. Our bet was simple: if he could learn to translate his boundary-thinking into language that clients could follow, he wouldn't become a normal consultant. He would become something better, because he already saw systems more clearly than the rest of us. He just needed to learn how to bring others along.

That bet paid off. Today he's one of our strongest people, not because he became more like everyone else, but because he learned to express his difference in ways that clients could use. The four-eyed monster needs people like him—people who see what others miss—but it also needs a system that doesn't reject them before they've had a chance to grow.

## CONTRAST_SERIES

**Curiosity versus boundary-thinking.**

At the beginning of 2024, I thought the future consultant was simply a curious consultant—the kind who tries things because they have to, not because anyone told them to. I still believe curiosity matters enormously. Consulting has long attracted people who drink the firm Kool-Aid, who feel like they know more than their clients about some mysterious strategy concept but couldn't articulate what exactly they know. That kind of consultant is dying. The future belongs to people who learn faster than anyone else.

But my view has evolved. Curiosity is necessary and insufficient.

Consider a contact center AI strategy, where a large portion of the work involves intent detection—understanding what customers are trying to accomplish when they call or message. A curious consultant gets excited about how intents work, reads up on the latest approaches, experiments with different models. But a boundary-thinking consultant sees something deeper: that every intent is an atomic organizing principle, that the intents don't just describe customer behavior but structure everything downstream, that if you get the intent architecture wrong, nothing else you build will compensate for it. The boundary-thinker sees the problem as a system of atoms forming an organism, and once you see it that way, the path to building the whole thing becomes visible.

Most consultants will build an intent list just to have one—another deliverable to add to the deck. Curiosity makes them more enthusiastic about this work, but it doesn't stop them from losing sight of why the work matters. Curiosity for its own sake produces passionate people who are delightful to talk to and seem impressively up-to-date on AI news. But that alone doesn't move the needle for clients who need systems that actually work.

**Constraints versus problems.**

Take the boundary idea further: ignoring it entirely is what happens when you produce the same strategy deck everyone has seen a million times. Some introduction. Some benchmarking. Some recommendations. A roadmap with optimistic timelines. Value projections that no one quite believes.

But when you dissect the boundaries first, strategy takes on a different shape. You start asking questions that have specific answers: how long will it take to build the context needed for each intent? How should intents be organized so they can evolve as the business changes? What would it take to add temporal context, to incorporate event streams that give the system memory? All of this can be phased into a roadmap that corresponds to reality—a strategy for bringing something to life rather than a document that performs the appearance of strategy.

Some will object that their work is higher-level, that they're transforming the whole enterprise rather than building a single system. But enterprise transformation requires the same thinking, just at a different scale. Now your constraints are the competitive forces shaping the entire business. How do you phase AI initiatives to address those forces in an order that makes sense? How do you design solutions that move the needle on what actually matters? Without boundary-thinking, the answer is always another deck full of boxes and arrows. With it, the answer is a plan that could actually be executed.

**Boring versus shiny.**

You might think that everything I'm describing makes consulting sound tedious. But there's only one thing that's truly boring in this work: building something shiny that nobody uses.

The most seemingly tedious task becomes rewarding the moment it reaches users who depend on it. My early AI work was in chemical plants and refineries—developing predictive monitoring models using neural networks, statistical methods, and rule-based systems. Large programs doing genuinely novel things. But what we lacked, for years, was the boring part: embedding these models into interfaces simple enough that operators could use them without thinking about "AI" at all. They needed to see what the model was telling them as naturally as they saw temperature readings or pressure gauges.

It took sitting with a brilliant operator-turned-engineer to understand what we'd been missing. After years of poor adoption, we built a new interface—stripped down, almost stupidly simple—and put it in the control room. Operators started using it overnight. No elaborate change management campaign, no training programs. Just something that fit into how they already worked. The complex model underneath was exactly the same. What changed was the boring part: how it showed up in their day.

Shiny things that sit unused are the most boring outcome in consulting. Simple things that become part of how people work are where the satisfaction lives.

**Learning versus knowing.**

The irony of hiring for learning in AI is that the best learners are often not the ones who seem most up-to-date. They're not the ones dabbling with every new tool or breathlessly tracking every announcement. They've found a core set of tools they trust, and they've gone deep—learning not just how to use those tools but how to extend them, how to know when they're the wrong choice, how to build things that last.

Some might call this focus. I think it's something more: an understanding that real learning means prioritizing depth over coverage, the heart of a subject over its surface. People who learn this way become teachers, and teaching accelerates learning in ways that merely consuming information never does. Our strongest consultants are the ones who can explain what they know to others, not because we require it, but because the act of explaining forces a clarity that nothing else provides.

The firms that hire for credentials are betting on what people already know. We've learned to bet on how fast people can come to know what they need to—and whether they can bring others along once they do.

## ACTIONABLE_ADVICE

We hear endless talk about the collapsing consulting pyramid—how junior people are becoming less relevant while certain senior ones become more productive, how the layers in between may be the most at risk. I find most of this analysis shallow. Consulting has always been a people-centric business, and the pyramid exists because developing people takes time and structure. The question isn't whether the pyramid will survive, but how its shape will change as the work itself changes.

What's undeniable is that AI is forcing roles to shift. Data scientists are becoming AI systems engineers, but not every experienced data scientist can make that transition, and not every new hire has the support they need to develop in this direction. Different people need different scaffolding. The idea that you can drop someone onto a project and expect them to figure everything out—that apprenticeship is somehow obsolete—misunderstands what makes consulting teams work.

So how do you rethink the pyramid while everything else is in flux? My answer: you need unifying platforms. Not slide decks as offerings, but genuine systems—technical and methodological—that give people at every level something concrete to work on together. The vine that connects the branches.

This sounds idealistic, and it is. I don't think we arrived here through pure intention. We had to scrap, to find whatever footholds we could. When we were ten people, we unified around industrial AI—predictive models for physical systems. When we grew to fifty, we expanded to data governance programs that had nothing to do with industrial applications but everything to do with our emerging point of view on how to make AI useful in practice.

Eventually this scrapping led to something coherent: an approach to bringing enterprise-grade AI to life that combines what we now call AI activation, context architecture, and AI systems engineering. But what makes it work isn't any one technology or framework. It's how the pieces fit together—how the people at each level can see their work connecting to something larger. The orchestration is the platform.

What's remarkable about a functioning consulting firm is that you can't replicate it by studying its assets or org chart. What makes it work is embedded in the system itself—the fine-tuned components, yes, but also the spirit, the shared conviction that we're building something no one else has done for a client. The monster's four eyes have to believe they're looking at the same thing.

## OBJECTION_ANTICIPATED

The nature of any consulting firm is a hybrid focus on sales and delivery. When we first articulated our approach, there was little interest from leadership. Part of the problem was language: when we talked about "context engineering," people heard it as rebranded "prompt engineering" and dismissed it accordingly.

This taught us something about how new ideas spread inside firms. You have to solve two problems at once: building the actual system, and finding words that make the system legible to people who haven't thought about it as deeply as you have. The translation problem isn't separate from the work—it is part of the work. And when you solve it, something interesting happens: explaining becomes exciting. Every skeptical question becomes an invitation to go deeper, and that enthusiasm is visible. People can sense when you've thought about something far more than they have, and most of them will defer rather than challenge at a level they're not prepared for.

But the harder objection I've faced is about my own limitations. I used to think I had some intuitive gift for evaluating candidates—a sense for who would fit, who would grow, who would surprise us. I've come to believe this is largely self-deception. Humans are too complex to evaluate on vibes, and yet vibes are how most of us make hiring decisions.

The research is clear: Schmidt and Hunter's meta-analysis found that structured interviews substantially outperform unstructured ones. My problem is that I think in systems, so I'm drawn to people who add something new to the system even when they have obvious gaps. But consulting teams aren't purely mechanical systems, or even biological systems where each organism fills a specialized niche. They're social-professional systems, and there's always a thick core layer of skills—communication, client presence, basic reliability—that people have to master before their unique strengths become valuable.

This is why we're moving toward more structured approaches, even as we try to preserve room for the outliers who don't fit standard patterns. And it's why diversity matters so much more than most firms acknowledge. BCG found that companies with above-average leadership diversity report innovation revenue nearly twenty points higher than those with below-average diversity. Well-managed diverse teams consider more alternatives and make better decisions. But poorly managed diverse teams suffer from misunderstandings and slow consensus. The question isn't whether diversity helps—it's whether you've built a system that can actually use it.

Over time, I hope we can lower the threshold of the core layer and move toward something more like a rainforest: a rich ecosystem of specialists connected by shared infrastructure rather than uniform training. But that requires team design far beyond what most firms have achieved, including us.

## CLOSE_WITH_GIFT

What still surprises me about AI consulting is that it's the most human form of consulting there is.

No subject touches as much fear, confusion, excitement, and linguistic chaos as AI does right now. And no form of consulting requires such careful work to embed human knowledge into systems—a task that resembles anthropology more than engineering, that forces you to understand how people actually think and decide and know. When it's done well, no other consulting creates value as immediately visible to clients. They can see their own knowledge coming back to them, structured and extended in ways they couldn't have done alone.

Ben Horowitz wrote about something he called The Struggle: "The Struggle is when you wonder why you started the company in the first place. The Struggle is when people ask you why you don't quit and you don't know the answer. The Struggle is when your employees think you are lying and you think they may be right. The Struggle is when food loses its taste... The Struggle is not failure, but it causes failure. Especially if you are weak."

Building an AI consulting team is The Struggle. It's when you wonder why you're doing this at all, when the four-eyed monster refuses to cohere, when the people you bet on stumble and you have to decide whether to help them through or let them go.

But here's what I didn't expect: the struggle is where you find the people who matter. Not because suffering is noble, but because the struggle reveals who can see problems fresh, who cares about boundaries rather than just content, who makes the boring work so that the complex work actually functions.

You don't find those people by looking for consultants who look like consultants. You find them by building a system that lets them develop their horns after they arrive—a system with room for apprenticeship, with patience for translation, with belief that the four eyes can learn to see as one.

That's the uncomfortable truth about AI consulting: none of us know how to hire for what we actually need. We're all still learning what the new shape of consulting looks like, still building the plane while we fly it. The teams that will win are the ones honest enough to admit this—and structured enough to get better anyway.
