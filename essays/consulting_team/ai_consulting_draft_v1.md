# The Four-Eyed Monster

## SITUATION_SETUP

Paul Graham writes that startups take off because founders make them take off. There may be a handful that grew by themselves, but usually it takes some sort of push. A good metaphor, he says, is the crank that car engines had before electric starters. Once the engine was going, it would keep going, but there was a separate and laborious process to get it going.

Building an AI consulting team works the same way. Ours didn't magically appear. We didn't start as an "AI team." We didn't even start as a "data team." We were just solving problems—real problems, right in front of us. Optimizing data centers. Nuclear shipyards. Chemical plants. Our people were getting patents on work they'd never done before. Cool stuff. Hard stuff.

But what we lacked was the thing that turns a group of people into a team: some cohesion beyond the mechanics of the work itself. And this, I've come to believe, is the core challenge of all consulting—not just AI consulting. Consulting is a four-eyed monster. Client projects. Cohesive assets. Collective knowledge sharing. Diversity of skills. Maybe more than any other industry, these are all part of the same system, and when it's working, the only thing you see is all four eyes merging into one.

AI teams are no different. But they do raise a question that other consulting practices can ignore: why build one at all? What's the missing problem?

Anyone who's a consultant—a true consultant—knows that consulting is about helping clients solve something hard and watching a team work in a focused, strange, only-in-consulting way to get it done. Early AI teams were either software development shops or data science shops. Where was the team that could truly break down how AI fits in the enterprise? That could dissect all the fine-tuned business physics—rules, domain know-how, everything needed to make a decision? That's the team I wanted to build.

## EXPECTED_ADVICE_REJECTED

The standard advice for building any consulting team is to hire people who look like consultants. Polish. Presence. The ability to fill a room with confidence even when you don't know what you're talking about. For AI teams specifically, the advice mutates slightly: hire AI experts. Get the credentials. Poach the data scientists with publication records.

I used to believe this. I was far, far too optimistic about people with the right backgrounds. My inclination was to look for things they did well and assume the rest would follow. It's a bit like dating someone with obvious problems and thinking you can fix them—if there's some intuitive hunch, some spark, we can make it work.

Here's what I've learned: my intuitive hiring guesses have been horribly wrong. But they've also been wonderfully right. And after all my experience, I'm not sure I'd change much, because the strength of the rights far outweighs the pain of the wrongs.

What I would change is what I'm looking for.

I see it constantly in consulting: people hiring people who feel just like them. Same background. Same ways of working. Same corporate blandness. I'd rather struggle with all the gambles for the few who break this mold.

One of the great things about being a consulting leader is getting to see how little you know. I am a horrible manager of project details, yet our team would quickly turn from an ever-growing pyramid to a pile of rocks without the people who are good at that. My client skills are unique in understanding problems, but nothing special in being someone clients love without substance—no firm exists without those people either. But what I want to write about is the less-discussed scaffolding of AI consulting. And maybe the least-discussed part of all: good AI consulting is no different from the best practices of classical consulting.

## REFRAMING_PREMISE

Where we've gotten better at hiring is when we've made gut feelings concrete. Actual criteria you can point to.

One criterion has not led us astray in the AI age: the desire to hire the raw curious. People who will get up early or stay late for no reason other than to learn. Not because anyone told them. Not because some rule said they should. Just because they had to.

The ability to learn while building is likely the most important trait on our team. And the benefit of this skill is compounding. These people can start off being the most lost, the most confused. Then they get a little better. Then a little better, to the point where they start to look okay. Then a little better, where they start performing well. Then they become the unicorn you'll never be able to hire from outside.

The risk and investment in people who naturally learn is always worth it. It's the best way to de-risk a hire.

But I've realized something that surprised me. Curiosity is a secondary skill to something even more important, and perhaps even less common. It's not technical. It's not AI-specific.

It's the ability to see the boundaries of a problem and do something with them.

Call it boundary-thinking. It's an abstractly mathematical way of viewing things—being able to look at an AI system and intuitively understand where to focus, what's most important, what actually matters. Most consultants, even today, go down the pathway of building deliverables just to do it. They frame problems aligned to what they did before, not even remembering how they started to do it in the first place. Curiosity alone will only make them more enthusiastic about this approach.

The hiring practice that never fails is finding people who can't think that way even if you asked them to. They can't, because their mind never sees the same problem as the same problem. They reconstruct the problem system uniquely every time, then spend most of their time thinking about the parts that actually matter.

## MECHANISM_REVEALED

Let me give you an example.

One of my best consultants was an engineer. When he came through our hiring program, the feedback said things like "doesn't communicate well" and "slide deck skills are below par." They had him preranked as the lowest consultant in that junior class.

Then I pulled him onto my program and gave him a challenge. Stop building decks just to build decks. Think about what you need to communicate and figure out how to communicate it in one or two slides. Find evidence. Find numbers. Find what you need to get people satisfied with just those slides. Do that every time.

I've had many people come through my programs who completely fail at this. The one or two slides never progress beyond the twenty-slide decks of generic jargon. But this consultant found a way to do it.

Why? And why did he struggle when he played the game against the generic consultants?

Perhaps he was thinking about problem-centricity all along, but was put into an authoritarian learning environment that told him good looked like something that wasn't mapped to how he thought. Good was an output that looked polished but didn't say anything distinctive. Problem-thinkers find distinctive in the brevity, in the thought. They're rare. And they're rarer still because our processes weed them out.

We kill the unicorns right in front of us because we expect unicorns to come with horns already visible.

The real question with this consultant wasn't "is he good enough?" It was: do we let the system spit him out, or do we treat him like an apprentice and help him get over the wall? Our bet was simple. If he could get through that first wall—learning to communicate, to frame problems, to survive a client meeting without losing the depth of his thinking—he wouldn't just become a normal consultant. He'd become a super-consultant. Because he already saw the system more deeply than the rest of us.

## CONTRAST_SERIES

**Curiosity versus boundary-thinking.**

At the beginning of 2024, I thought the future consultant was a curious consultant. The kind who tries things just because they have to, not because anyone told them, and certainly not because some arbitrary rule said they couldn't. I still believe this is the future. It's a huge shift, because I don't think consulting is organically attractive to curious people. More than anywhere else, consulting is where people go to drink serious firm Kool-Aid. Where people go to feel like they know more than their clients about some mysterious strategy concept but couldn't ever articulate exactly what they know more about. It's the feeling of knowing more just because they're consultants—not because they've observed some intellectual difference.

I believe that form of consultant is dying. The one who learns faster than anyone is the future.

But my view has evolved. Curiosity is secondary to something harder to find.

Take a contact center AI strategy. A big portion will be working around intent detection. A curious consultant gets excited about how intents work. A boundary-thinker understands that intents have far more weight in the system than just their definitions. Every intent can be an atomic unifying force—you start to see the problem as a bunch of atoms that make an organism, and suddenly there's a clear path to how to build the whole thing.

Most consultants will go down the pathway of building an intent list just to do it. Curiosity makes them more enthusiastic about it. But they still lose track of the boundaries. Curiosity for curiosity's sake creates passionate people who are great to talk to, who seem on top of their AI news game. But that alone doesn't move the needle for our clients.

**Constraints versus problems.**

If we take this boundary idea further: ignoring it entirely is what happens when you build the same exact strategy deck we've all seen a million times. Some intro. Some benchmarking. Some recommendations. A roadmap. Value numbers that don't seem feasible.

But by dissecting the boundaries first, you see strategy in a new light. Everything needed to bring that contact center AI to life—how much time will it take to build out the context aligned to the intents? How to align intents in a way that makes sense? How to extend the system to do more—maybe add temporal context with event streaming? All of this can then be phased into a real actionable roadmap. A real estimate of phased value. A real strategy to bring it to life.

You might say, "My strategy is higher level—I change the whole enterprise." But you don't if your roadmap looks like the generic flow. Changing at the enterprise level requires the same thinking—except now your constraints are the core competitive forces of the enterprise. How do you phase AI prioritization to align to alleviating those forces? How do you envision solutions that truly move the needle?

**Boring versus shiny.**

In all of this, you might think I believe consulting is boring.

There's only one boring thing in consulting: building something shiny that never gets used.

The most boring task is the most rewarding if it's in the hands of users. My early days of AI were in chemical plants and refining. We were developing predictive monitoring models—neural nets, statistical methods, rule-based monitoring. Large programs. Cool stuff. But what we lacked was the boring part: embedding these models into simple, stupid-simple sometimes, interfaces that operators could use in a way that fit their workflow.

It took sitting down with a brilliant operator-turned-engineer to understand how to build this right. After years of poor adoption, we added the new simple interface in the control room and operators started using it overnight. We didn't need to throw additional change management at it. Just simple checks to see how it was working.

It sounds stupid and boring, but boring makes the complex work. Shiny for shiny's sake makes nothing work. Shiny is so boring.

**Learning versus knowing.**

We hired someone the firm didn't trust. Deep in industrial data science and systems, almost no consulting polish. The default move in consulting is to throw people like that straight onto a project, judge them on how fast they sound like everyone else, and, when they stumble, quietly decide they're "not a fit."

That's exactly what happened at first. We put him on a live project, he got harsh feedback from managers who had no patience for his learning curve, and everyone could see the gap between how he thought and how the firm expected him to behave.

The real question wasn't "is he good enough?" It was: do we let the system spit him out or treat him like an apprentice and help him over the wall?

Our bet was simple: if he could get through that first wall—learning to communicate, to frame problems, to survive a client meeting without losing the depth of his thinking—he wouldn't just become a normal consultant. He'd become a super-consultant. Because he already saw the system more deeply than the rest of us.

What does it mean to be a learning employee? It means someone who isn't afraid to start doing something and keep going when they don't know the answers. They find answers themselves with enough work. And they can clearly articulate what they learned and share it. People who teach others demonstrate learning more than anyone—and get far more from the teaching process than even the people they teach.

The irony with learning employees in AI is that they're not the ones up to speed on every market change. They're not dabbling with every tool. They've found a core toolset and they explore ways to get things done within it. They trust it—yet they're willing to branch outside when they need to. Some might call this focus. I think it's something deeper: a desire to prioritize the heart over the skin of topics.

## ACTIONABLE_ADVICE

We hear a lot about the collapsing pyramid in consulting. How maybe junior people are less relevant, yet certain ones can be more productive. How seniors are less useful. How the people in the middle are the most dangerous.

I find this completely bullshit. Consulting at its core is a people-centric business. It's the pyramid that brings it to life.

Still, there's a shifting gap as we recreate roles in AI. Data scientists shift to AI systems engineers. Not every experienced data scientist can make this shift. Not every junior AI systems engineer feels supported enough to make it. Different hires need different support systems. Throwing them on a barren island and expecting them to build a ship is a failing idea.

So how do we rethink the pyramid to ensure junior people get the right development support while the rest of it is in flux? That's the core problem of the new AI consulting firm.

It's less about what junior people can't do. It's more about what you need to structurally change. My idea: you need unifying platforms. Get away from the "slide deck as an offering." You need a full system that everyone can latch onto. The vine to connect the branches. Complex systems that show how these roles can work together, solve problems together, evolve together.

This sounds idealistic because it is. I don't think we got here intentionally. We had to scrap. When we had ten people, we found a small program unified around industrial AI and focused on that. When we had fifty, we expanded to data governance programs as well. We scraped by with whatever we could, but we constantly unified around a differentiated vision for how to make AI work in practice.

Eventually this led to our unified approach for bringing enterprise-grade AI to life. Orchestrated programs that bring elements of design—we now call it AI activation—context architecture, and AI systems engineering together in a way where we systematically build out intents and back each intent with all of the business and domain know-how.

What makes our team work isn't a magic technology or architectural idea or some differentiated business experience. It's the orchestration of the people to build a system that constantly gets business buy-in, working at the interface of the business to extract all the accumulated know-how and build disciplined intelligence.

What's amazing about a functioning consulting firm is that this can't be replicated by seeing our assets or our team structure or even our plan. It's embedded in the workings of the system. Part of it is fine-tuned components. Another part is the spirit and passion and understanding that we're building something no one else has done for a client.

## OBJECTION_ANTICIPATED

The nature of any firm is the hybrid focus on sales and delivery. When we first brought our thinking to life, there was little interest in what we were doing.

Part of this was our language. When we say things like "context engineering," leaders push back thinking it's just rebranded "prompt engineering." How do we simultaneously figure out how to get the orchestrated system in place while also figuring out the branding, the language, the ways of talking about it?

Here's what I've learned: in doing so, we push ourselves to get excited about it. It makes explaining it exciting. Every challenge is met with so much enthusiasm that the person challenging instantly collapses—out of an emotionally aware sense that you have thought far more about this than they have, and challenging you will require more depth than they're willing to bring.

But there's a harder objection I've had to face: I'm not the best person to make hiring decisions.

I used to think I was. I thought I had some intuitive strength for understanding whether a candidate would fit. I've realized this is mostly bullshit. Humans are too complex to understand on an intuitive level, yet it's the way most of us approach interviews. The data supports that we suck at trusting vibes: Schmidt and Hunter's meta-analysis shows structured interviews dramatically outperform unstructured ones.

My problem is that I think in systems. I try to find people who add color to the system, even if there are obvious gaps. But a consulting team isn't like a mechanical system, or even a biological system where each organism has evolved a specialist role. A consulting team is a social-professional system. There's always a thick core layer that any employee must get through before their unique specialist skills can fit in.

This is why we're evolving more structured interview approaches. We're still learning. But I've also learned to balance this with the power of diversity. BCG's study of 1,700 companies found that companies with above-average leadership diversity report innovation revenue 19 percentage points higher than those with below-average diversity. A complementary meta-study found that well-managed diverse teams process information 31% more thoroughly, consider 47% more alternative solutions, and make 87% higher-quality decisions.

The catch: poorly managed diverse teams experience 56% more misunderstandings and 73% longer consensus times.

So the question becomes: how do you balance both? It comes down to how your team is set up to leverage diversity—to leverage the talents that lie outside the thick core layer. Many consulting companies overindex on the core layer and lose the benefits of innovation and diversity. Seeing those benefits requires a system designed to leverage them properly.

It's an evolving challenge. I hope over time we can lower the core layer threshold and move closer to a rainforest of specialists. But it will require team system design far beyond what we currently have—or what any consulting team currently has.

## CLOSE_WITH_GIFT

What still surprises me about AI consulting is that it's the most human form of consulting there is.

No subject touches fear or confusion or excitement or cluttered language the way AI does right now. And no form of consulting requires new ways of embedding knowledge into AI systems—a task much more like human knowledge engineering than artificial knowledge engineering. And no form of consulting, when delivered well, is as immediately valuable to our clients.

Ben Horowitz writes about The Struggle:

"The Struggle is when you wonder why you started the company in the first place. The Struggle is when people ask you why you don't quit and you don't know the answer. The Struggle is when your employees think you are lying and you think they may be right. The Struggle is when food loses its taste... The Struggle is not failure, but it causes failure. Especially if you are weak."

Building an AI consulting team is The Struggle. It's when you wonder why you're doing this at all. It's when the four-eyed monster refuses to merge into one. It's when the people you bet on stumble and you have to decide whether to let the system spit them out or help them over the wall.

But here's what I didn't expect: the struggle is the point.

Not because suffering is noble. Because the struggle is where you find the people who reconstruct every problem from scratch. The ones who see boundaries others don't. The ones who make the boring work so the complex works. The ones who learn faster than the market changes.

You don't find them by looking for consultants who look like consultants. You find them by building a system that lets unicorns grow their horns after they arrive.

That's the uncomfortable truth about AI consulting: we don't know how to hire for what we actually need. We're all still learning. And the teams that will win are the ones honest enough to admit that—and structured enough to keep getting better at it anyway.
