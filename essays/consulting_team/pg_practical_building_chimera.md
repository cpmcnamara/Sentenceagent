# How to Build the Chimera

## MISCONCEPTION_STATED

The way to build an AI consulting team is not to hire AI experts.

That sounds backwards. Conventional wisdom says you need people who understand the technology—data scientists, ML engineers, people who can explain transformers and fine-tuning. The assumption is that the hard problems are technical, and technical problems require technical people.

But that's not what actually separates teams that deliver from teams that don't. The hardest problems in AI consulting aren't technical or business problems. They're creative design challenges. And the people who solve creative design challenges aren't necessarily the ones with the deepest technical knowledge. They're the ones with curiosity, constraints-obsession, and the ability to get excited about building blocks instead of shininess.

## BETTER_MODEL

Here's the model that actually works: you're building a chimera.

A chimera isn't a collection of specialists—it's a single organism made of different parts that work together. The technical people need business intuition. The business people need technical literacy. Everyone needs creative design instincts. And most importantly, everyone needs to be a learner, not just an expert.

The better model separates three things that most teams conflate:

**Capability vs. Curiosity**: Technical capability is table stakes, but curiosity is the differentiator. There's a confidence problem where non-technical people think "AI people" are different from them. They're not. The AI people who succeed are the ones who dive in with curiosity, regardless of their starting point.

**Problems vs. Constraints**: Most teams focus on solving problems. But constraints are what make an AI system actually work. You need people who are obsessed with constraints—who get excited about the limitations, the edge cases, the boring stuff that determines whether something will work in production.

**Shininess vs. Blocks**: The visible AI—the demo, the interface, the impressive output—is the shininess. The building blocks—data foundations, governance, integration—are what makes it real. You need people who get excited about blocks, not just shininess.

## FILTER_CRITERIA

How do you know if you're building the right team?

**The curiosity test**: When someone encounters something they don't understand, do they retreat or advance? The people you want lean forward. They're not intimidated by unfamiliar territory. This matters more than credentials.

**The constraints test**: Ask a candidate what excited them most about a recent project. If they talk about the clever solution, that's fine. If they talk about the gnarly constraint they navigated—the data that wasn't there, the integration that almost didn't work, the edge case that nearly broke everything—that's better.

**The blocks test**: Do they get excited about data governance? If the answer is "not really," that's a red flag. The next-gen team needs people who see data foundations not as overhead but as the foundation of a larger "Context Engineering" capability.

**The learning test**: Classical engineers tend to be better in this world than pure strategists, because engineers need on-the-ground reality and structures. But the real test is whether someone's desire to learn outpaces the market. If they stop learning, they stop being useful.

## HOW_TO_NOTICE

How do you develop the skill of spotting these people?

Look for the non-obvious path. Hiring out of school often makes more sense than hiring experienced professionals—not because experience is bad, but because experienced people often carry bad habits and preconceptions. Someone fresh can be shaped by your culture rather than fighting against their old one.

Notice who asks about the boring parts. In interviews, everyone wants to talk about the exciting AI applications. The people you want are the ones who ask: "How does this connect to your data infrastructure?" or "What happens when the model is wrong?"

Watch for friction with experience. Surprisingly, there's pushback to change even from teams that recommend change to clients. People with classical management consulting backgrounds often struggle because AI teams must reinvent fast. This isn't a character flaw—it's a pattern to recognize.

## WELLS_IDENTIFIED

Where do you look for these people?

**New graduates with technical curiosity**: Not necessarily CS majors. People who have learned to code on their own, who built something weird on the side, who are excited rather than intimidated by unfamiliar technical territory.

**Engineers who want to think bigger**: Classical engineers who feel constrained by pure execution. They want to solve business problems but they have the discipline of building things that work.

**Domain experts who've gotten their hands dirty**: People from industry who understand a specific domain deeply and have already started experimenting with AI applications. They bring the constraints you need to understand.

**Consultants who are honest about AI**: Most AI consulting is bullshit. Clients are desperate for people who "get it." The consultants who can tell stories that aren't BS—who admit what doesn't work and focus on what does—are rare and valuable.

## RECIPES

Here's how to actually build the team:

**Recipe 1: Deep First, Then Broaden**

Start with one differentiated vision. We started with "Intelligent Industry"—going deep in a specific domain first. You build credibility and capability in a narrow area, then broaden by connecting new work to that deep core. Deep but interconnected, not shallow and scattered.

**Recipe 2: Awesome Projects as Retention**

The unifying force that prevents people from leaving is awesome projects. Not compensation, not titles—projects. Make sure the best people are on the most interesting problems. If someone's desire to learn is outpacing what you can offer them, you'll lose them. So make sure you can keep up.

**Recipe 3: Define Moments, Not Problems**

Build systems around "defining moments" with clients. Don't create problems that don't exist. Instead, identify the moments where AI can genuinely change something—and structure your team to deliver at those moments.

**Recipe 4: Pair Operations with Vision**

Operations are as important as vision. You can have the best ideas in the world, but you can't get from 0 to 1 without operational excellence. Every visionary needs an operator. Build that into your team structure from the start.

**Recipe 5: Reframe the Boring**

Get people excited about the boring stuff. Data governance sounds dull until you call it "Context Engineering." The reframe matters. The work is the same, but how people feel about the work determines whether they do it well.

## WARNING_SIGNS

You're doing it wrong if:

**You're hiring for credentials over curiosity.** If your job descriptions emphasize degrees and years of experience over learning velocity and constraints-thinking, you're selecting for the wrong things.

**Everyone wants to own AI.** This is the opposite of the data governance problem, where no one wanted ownership. When everyone wants to own AI, you need a "new definition of help"—one where ownership is shared and contribution is measured differently.

**Your technical people and business people don't talk.** The chimera only works if the parts are connected. If your data scientists don't understand the business problem and your consultants don't understand the technical constraints, you have specialists, not a team.

**Your experienced people are resisting change.** Friction from experience is a signal. If your most valuable teammates with classical backgrounds are pushing back on reinvention, you have a culture problem that will slow everything down.

## CLOSE_PRACTICAL

The chimera isn't a mythological creature. It's the only team structure that works.

Start with one person who has the hustle—who can go from nothing to something through sheer will and curiosity. Build around them with people who get excited about constraints, building blocks, and boring problems. Go deep in one domain before you broaden. Use awesome projects to keep people, not promises. Pair every visionary with an operator.

Most AI consulting fails because teams are built around what looks impressive rather than what actually works. The clients who matter can tell the difference. They're desperate for people who get it.

Be the team that gets it.
