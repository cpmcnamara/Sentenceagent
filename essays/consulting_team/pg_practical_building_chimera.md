# How to Build the Chimera

## MISCONCEPTION_STATED

The way to build an AI consulting team is not to hire AI experts.

I know. I spent three years hiring AI experts and watching them fail. Data scientists who could explain attention mechanisms but couldn't explain why a client should care. ML engineers who built beautiful models that sat on laptops, never deployed. "AI strategists" whose strategies were repackaged McKinsey slides with the word "AI" inserted.

The assumption is that the hard problems are technical. Get people who understand transformers, fine-tuning, RAG architectures. The technology is complicated, so hire technologists.

That's not what separates teams that deliver from teams that don't.

## BETTER_MODEL

Here's what actually works: you're building a chimera.

Not a collection of specialists. A single organism where every part can see the whole problem. The technical people need business intuition. The business people need to get their hands dirty with data. Everyone needs creative design instincts.

Why does this work when specialist teams fail? Because AI problems don't respect boundaries. A model that works technically but doesn't fit the workflow is demo-ware. A strategy that sounds good but ignores data constraints is theater. I watched a $2M computer vision project die because nobody on the team had asked where the training images would come from. They had three PhDs in deep learning. None of them had talked to the operations team that would need to capture the images.

The chimera works because it asks the obvious questions that specialists miss.

Three distinctions that most teams conflate:

**Capability vs. Curiosity.** Capability tells you what someone can do today. Curiosity predicts whether they'll learn what matters tomorrow. AI changes fast enough that today's expertise is next year's liability. I've started asking candidates about the last thing that confused them. The good ones light up. The bad ones get defensive.

**Problems vs. Constraints.** "Build a recommendation engine" is a problem. "Build a recommendation engine that works with 30% missing data, runs in 200ms, and can't access customer PII" is a design challenge. The constraints aren't obstacles—they're the specification. Most teams focus on the problem statement. The teams that ship focus on constraints.

**Shininess vs. Blocks.** The demo gets applause. The data pipeline gets deployed. You can fake shininess. You can't fake blocks. When I interview candidates who've "worked on AI projects," I ask about the data governance. If they can't explain it, they were decoration.

## FILTER_CRITERIA

Four tests identify the right people.

**The curiosity test.** When someone encounters something unfamiliar, do they retreat or advance? Show them something they don't understand. The people you want ask questions. The people you don't want change the subject. This matters more than credentials.

**The constraints test.** Ask what excited them about a recent project. If they talk about the clever solution—fine. If they talk about the gnarly constraint, the data that wasn't there, the edge case that nearly broke everything—better. The constraint-obsessed are rare. Hire them.

**The blocks test.** Do they get excited about data governance? I've started calling it "context engineering" because the frame matters. Same work, different energy. But if someone hears "data governance" and their eyes glaze over, they're a liability. The boring stuff determines success.

**The learning test.** Is their desire to learn outpacing the market? Engineering backgrounds help here—engineering culture assumes you'll learn on the job. Pure strategists often struggle. They're used to knowing. AI requires constant not-knowing.

## HOW_TO_NOTICE

Spotting these people requires unlearning how you normally hire.

New graduates beat experienced hires more often than I expected. Not because experience is bad, but because experienced consultants carry habits from a world that doesn't exist anymore. Someone with ten years in traditional consulting has ten years of "how things are done" to unlearn. Someone fresh can be shaped by your culture.

Notice who asks about the boring parts. In interviews, everyone wants to talk about exciting AI applications. The people you want ask about data infrastructure, failure modes, deployment pipelines. One candidate spent half our interview asking about our data quality processes. I hired her immediately. She was the only person who cared about the thing that determines whether projects succeed.

Watch for friction with experience. This surprised me: even people who recommend change to clients resist change themselves. Classical management consulting backgrounds correlate with struggling in AI teams. It's not a character flaw—it's a pattern. AI teams must reinvent constantly. People from cultures that prize expertise over learning fight that reinvention.

## WELLS_IDENTIFIED

Four wells produce the best hires.

**New graduates with technical curiosity.** Not necessarily CS majors. People who learned to code because they wanted to build something weird. Who taught themselves PyTorch on the side. Why this works: they don't carry habits from a world that doesn't exist. Fresh talent can be shaped. Learning velocity is easier to develop than to retrofit onto someone who thinks they already know.

**Engineers who want to think bigger.** Classical engineers who feel constrained by pure execution. They want business problems but have the discipline of building things that work. Why this works: engineering culture expects things to break. Engineers have structures for reality, not just ideas. They don't assume their models will work—they test.

**Domain experts who've gotten dirty.** People from industry who understand a domain deeply and have already experimented with AI. Why this works: they bring constraints you can't learn from outside. They know which edge cases kill adoption. They've already failed in ways that teach what to avoid.

**Consultants who admit what doesn't work.** Most AI consulting is bullshit. The consultants who say so—who focus on what actually works instead of what sounds impressive—are rare. Why this works: clients can tell. They've sat through too many promises. Someone who says "this won't work, but that will" beats ten who say "anything is possible."

## RECIPES

Five recipes for building the team.

**Recipe 1: Go deep first.** We started with "Intelligent Industry"—deep expertise in one domain. Build credibility there, then broaden by connecting new work to that core. Deep but interconnected beats shallow and scattered. The temptation is to go broad immediately. Resist it. You need a reputation before you can diversify.

**Recipe 2: Retain with projects, not perks.** The unifying force that keeps people is awesome projects. Not compensation, not titles—projects. If someone's desire to learn outpaces what you can offer them, you'll lose them. So make sure your best people are on your most interesting problems. The day I started worrying about whether projects were interesting enough was the day retention improved.

**Recipe 3: Define moments, not problems.** Don't create problems that don't exist. Identify the moments where AI can genuinely change something for a client—and structure your team to deliver at those moments. Most consultancies sell solutions. Sell transformation points instead.

**Recipe 4: Pair visionaries with operators.** Every visionary needs an operator. You can have the best ideas in the world, but you can't get from 0 to 1 without operational excellence. Build this pairing into your team structure from the start. Solo visionaries produce PowerPoints. Visionary-operator pairs produce products.

**Recipe 5: Reframe the boring.** Get people excited about unglamorous work. Data governance sounds dull until you call it "Context Engineering." The work is the same. How people feel about the work determines whether they do it well.

## WARNING_SIGNS

You're doing it wrong if:

**You're hiring for credentials over curiosity.** If your job descriptions emphasize degrees and years of experience over learning velocity and constraints-thinking, you're selecting for the wrong things.

**Everyone wants to own AI.** This is the opposite of the data governance problem, where no one wanted ownership. When everyone fights to own AI, you need a new definition of collaboration—one where contribution is measured differently than control.

**Your technical people and business people don't talk.** The chimera only works if the parts are connected. If your data scientists don't understand the business problem and your consultants can't read a data schema, you have specialists, not a team.

**Your experienced people resist reinvention.** Friction from experience is a signal. If your most senior people are pushing back on change, you have a culture problem. AI teams reinvent constantly. Resistance to reinvention is organizational death.

## CLOSE_PRACTICAL

Start with one person who asks about the data pipeline before asking about the model. Build around them.

That's the signal. That's who gets it.
