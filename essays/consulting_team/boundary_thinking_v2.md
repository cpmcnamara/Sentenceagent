# The Lowest-Ranked Consultant

The feedback on his interview read like a warning label. Doesn't communicate well. Slide deck skills below par. Seems uncomfortable in client settings. The firm's evaluation system had ranked him lowest in his cohort—dead last among twelve candidates who'd come through that quarter—and when I pulled up his file, I understood why. His sample deck was sparse to the point of seeming unfinished. His mock client presentation had gone silent in the middle when a senior partner asked him to explain his methodology. He had frozen, said "I don't know how to answer that in a way that sounds right," and the room had written him off.

I should have written him off too. The evaluation was clear, the consensus was unanimous, and I had seventeen other things demanding my attention that week. But something in his file nagged at me. His written analysis—a case study we give all candidates—was unlike anything I'd seen. Where other candidates produced the expected output, twenty slides of benchmarking and best practices and a roadmap with optimistic timelines, he had produced four slides. Just four. And those four slides contained more actual thinking than I typically saw in a hundred.

So I pulled him onto my program, against the advice of everyone who'd interviewed him. I told him to stop building decks just to build decks. Figure out what you actually need to communicate, I said, and find a way to do it in one or two slides. Find the evidence, the numbers, the core insight that would make those slides sufficient. Do that every time, no matter how much pressure you feel to pad it out.

I have given this challenge to eleven consultants since. Most fail it completely. They cannot escape the gravitational pull of the twenty-slide deck, the generic frameworks, the polished jargon that says nothing distinctive. They pad and expand because the system has taught them that volume signals effort, that coverage protects against criticism, that the safest thing is to include everything and let the client sort it out.

He was different. Within three months, his client reviews had shifted from "needs improvement" to "strongest analyst on the team." Within a year, partners who had voted against hiring him were requesting him for their programs. Today he runs one of our most complex engagements—not because he learned to be more like everyone else, but because he learned to express what made him different in ways clients could use.

---

I didn't understand what I'd seen in him until much later. At the time, I thought I was betting on raw intelligence, or maybe some vague sense of "potential." I was wrong. What I had actually seen—what made his four-slide analysis so striking—was something I now call boundary-thinking. The ability to look at a problem, see where its edges actually are, and spend effort only on the parts that will actually determine the outcome.

This sounds simple. It is not. Most consultants frame problems according to what they've done before. They see a new situation and immediately pattern-match to a familiar template. The energy sector client gets the energy sector deck. The contact center engagement gets the contact center framework. This isn't laziness—it's efficiency, or what feels like efficiency. But it means they never actually see the problem in front of them. They see the category the problem belongs to, and they execute the category's standard response.

Boundary-thinkers can't work this way. Their minds won't let them. They look at a contact center AI strategy and see that intent detection—understanding what customers are trying to accomplish—isn't just one component among many. It's the atomic organizing principle. Get the intent architecture wrong and nothing downstream compensates. They see this because they're not pattern-matching to "contact center engagements I've done before." They're looking at the actual system, finding where the constraints actually bind, asking which decisions will propagate through everything else.

The metaphor that helped me understand this came from an airport security line. If you want to optimize flow, the naive approach is to look at the length of the line and try to speed everything up. The experienced approach is to find the bottleneck—maybe the bag scanner, maybe the body scanner, maybe the ID check—and understand what's actually happening there. Once you debottleneck one constraint, you ask: where will the next one form? You're not optimizing "the line." You're understanding the system as a system of constraints, each one determining what's possible for everything downstream.

Most AI consulting gets this exactly backwards. It treats every constraint as a problem to be automated away, rather than a function to be understood. The consultant sees friction in a process and proposes removing it, without asking what that friction is actually doing—what decisions it's forcing, what errors it's catching, what knowledge is embedded in the human judgment that currently sits at that point. Boundary-thinkers ask different questions: What's actually embodied in this constraint? What happens if we change it? What new bottleneck forms when this one is resolved?

---

I wish I could say I understood this before I made expensive mistakes. I didn't. For years, I hired people based on domain experience—the theory being that someone who knew the industry would bridge the gap between what AI could do and what clients actually needed.

I hired two consultants specifically for their energy sector backgrounds. Both had spent years in the industry. Both understood the terminology, the physics, the way decisions got made. Both struggled. Not with domain knowledge—they had that in abundance—but with the actual work of deploying AI solutions. They knew what questions operators asked, but they couldn't see which questions actually governed the system's performance. They knew the vocabulary of the domain, but they couldn't translate between the AI system's logic and the human organization's needs. Meanwhile, our generalists—consultants with no energy experience at all—were outperforming them. They learned the domain by listening to stakeholders, and they saw the problem's boundaries without the baggage of assuming they already understood.

The lesson was uncomfortable: domain knowledge creates blindness as often as it creates insight. The expert sees what they expect to see. The boundary-thinker sees what's actually there.

---

If this sounds like I'm advocating for some mysterious quality that can't be assessed, I'm not. Boundary-thinking is visible in how people engage with problems, and you can see it in an interview if you know what to look for.

We give candidates a simple system with obvious inefficiency—a slow approval process, a backed-up pipeline—and ask them to diagnose it. Most jump immediately to solutions. They propose a technology before understanding the constraint. They treat the symptom as isolated rather than embedded in a flow. But occasionally, a candidate does something different. They ask where the slowdown actually occurs. They identify that fixing one bottleneck might create another. They want to understand the system before they propose changing it. That's boundary-thinking.

Or we give them a complex scenario with lots of data and ask them to make a two-slide presentation in thirty minutes. Most try to fit everything in. They shrink fonts, cram information, ask for more slides. The deck describes the situation but makes no claim. But occasionally, a candidate cuts. They identify the core question and answer it. The slides have an argument, not a summary. They made choices about what to leave out. That's boundary-thinking.

Neither exercise produces a "right" answer. That's the point. Boundary-thinkers reveal themselves through how they engage—the questions they ask, the parts they focus on, the complexity they shed rather than add.

---

Finding them is only half the problem. The harder half is not accidentally filtering them out.

The consultant I opened with—the one ranked lowest in his cohort—almost didn't make it past his first review. His client presence was still weak. His decks were still too sparse. A senior partner told me he seemed "unpolished," which was true, and "not ready for client exposure," which was also true. The evaluation system was doing what it was designed to do: identifying people who didn't fit the standard profile.

But the standard profile is wrong. The standard profile rewards polish over insight, coverage over depth, confidence over accuracy. The twenty-slide deck full of generic frameworks gets praise because it looks like what consultants are supposed to produce. The four-slide deck with an actual argument looks unfinished. The system selects for people who are good at performing consulting rather than people who are good at solving problems.

So we had a choice. Let the evaluation system spit him out—as it was designed to do, as it had done with who knows how many boundary-thinkers before him—or override the system and treat him as an apprentice. Give him time to develop the skills he lacked while protecting the skill he had.

We made the bet. It required a project lead who could articulate why he was valuable even when his outputs didn't look like what leadership expected. It required clear accountabilities—not just "improve your presentation skills" but specific gaps with specific plans: this meeting, practice this transition; this deck, get feedback on this structure. It required a norm that he could ask for help on the polish stuff without feeling like a failure, because his value wasn't in the polish.

It took eighteen months. By the end, he could hold a room, explain complex systems in language executives understood, build decks that were both sparse and complete. He hadn't become a different person. He had developed around his core difference rather than in spite of it.

---

Here's what I still get wrong: I believe I can spot boundary-thinkers intuitively, and I probably can't. I've made hires that felt right and weren't. I've passed on people who pattern-matched to failures and might have been exceptional. Human judgment is not as good as humans think it is, and mine is no exception.

What helps is making the intuition concrete. Not "I have a feeling about this candidate" but "I notice they asked about constraints before solutions in three separate questions." Not "they seem smart" but "their case analysis identified the governing bottleneck within ten minutes while other candidates never found it." The structured observation doesn't replace judgment—it gives judgment something to push against.

We've started scoring candidates on specific behaviors: Do they ask about constraints before solutions? Do they identify bottlenecks or focus on symptoms? Do they produce dense outputs or sprawling ones? Do they change their model when new information arrives, or do they patch it? None of this is perfect. But it's better than vibes.

---

The irony of building a team of boundary-thinkers is that you can't do it by optimizing for the metric that's easiest to measure. The polished candidates score well on every standard assessment. The ones who might be exceptional score badly on standard assessments and well on assessments that don't exist yet.

What I've learned is that most consulting systems are designed to do something other than what they claim. They claim to identify the best consultants. They actually identify the consultants who look most like the consultants who designed the system. The people who built the evaluation process optimized for people like themselves—because who else would they know how to recognize? And so the pipeline fills with people who are very good at seeming like consultants, while the genuine outliers get filtered out somewhere between the resume screen and the case interview.

The consultant I opened with is proof that this doesn't have to be true. He made it through by accident—a fluke of timing, a manager with a hunch, a willingness to override the system. That's not scalable. But it's where we start: with the admission that we don't actually know how to find the people we most need. With the commitment to build a system that's designed to be wrong, designed to surface people the existing system would have rejected, designed to give them time and scaffolding instead of expecting them to arrive fully formed.

The most valuable consultants aren't the most credentialed, or even the most curious. They're the ones who see problems differently—who can't pattern-match even when they try, because their minds won't stop looking for the edges. And most systems filter them out before they ever get a chance.

That's the problem. And I don't have a complete solution. But I've learned that the first step is admitting you're part of the system that's doing the filtering. Once you see that, you can start to build differently.

The four-slide deck was the tell. I almost missed it. How many times have I missed it entirely?
