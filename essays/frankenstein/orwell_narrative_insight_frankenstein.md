# The New Student

## SITUATIONAL_CONTEXT

In my second year of teaching, at a small liberal arts college in the Midwest, I was assigned a freshman seminar on Gothic literature. Fourteen students, a windowless basement classroom, Tuesday and Thursday afternoons. The college had accepted me because I needed the job and they needed someone willing to teach four sections at adjunct wages. I was not important enough to be hated, but not established enough to be respected. I existed in that peculiar academic limbo where you have authority over students but none over your own circumstances.

The seminar was meant to be discussion-based. I had arranged the desks in a circle because a book said to. We were reading *Frankenstein* first—the original, not the films—and I had prepared what I thought were penetrating questions about creation and responsibility.

## INTERNAL_CONFLICT_STATED

I had already made up my mind that I was a good teacher, or would become one. I believed in the transformative power of education, in the seminar as democratic space, in the text as common ground where differences could be bridged. This was the vocabulary I had learned in graduate school, and I believed it with the fervor of someone who had never tested it.

Theoretically I was on the side of inclusion, of making every student feel welcome. But secretly I divided the class into those who had done the reading and those who hadn't, those who contributed meaningfully and those who wasted time, those I enjoyed teaching and those I endured. With one part of my mind I believed all students deserved equal attention; with another part I resented the ones who didn't seem to want it.

## INCIDENT_ANNOUNCED

About three weeks into the semester, something happened which in a roundabout way was enlightening. A new student joined the class—a late add, transferred from another section that had been cancelled. It gave me a better glimpse than I had before of what the novel was actually about.

## RISING_ACTION

His name was David. He arrived the fourth Tuesday, a few minutes late, and took the only empty seat. He was large—not fat exactly, but oversized, as if he hadn't stopped growing when he should have. His clothes didn't quite fit. He had a skin condition I couldn't identify, red and flaking on his neck and cheeks. When he sat down, the students on either side shifted almost imperceptibly away.

I welcomed him to the class, made sure he had the syllabus, and we continued the discussion. We were in the middle of the creature's narrative—the part where he hides in the hovel next to the DeLacey cottage and learns language by watching the family. I asked about the creature's education: how does he learn, what does he learn, why does it matter?

David raised his hand. This was unusual—new students typically observe for a session or two before participating. His voice was unexpectedly high, not matching his size. He said the creature learns that language is power. The family talks about him as an absence—the monster out there, the danger—and he realizes that words create categories. Before language, he was just a being in the woods. After language, he was a monster.

It was a good observation. But something happened when he spoke. The other students didn't look at him. They looked at me, or at their books, or at the wall. A few exchanged glances I couldn't read. When I asked if anyone wanted to respond to David's point, there was a silence that had a different quality than the usual seminar silence. It wasn't thoughtful. It was waiting for something.

A student named Emily—one of my favorites, quick and well-read—raised her hand and made a completely different point, as if David hadn't spoken at all. The discussion continued. David didn't raise his hand again that day.

Over the next few weeks, the pattern established itself. David came to every class, did every reading, and occasionally offered comments that were at least as good as anyone else's. But something in the room resisted him. Students didn't respond to his points. They didn't sit near him if they could avoid it. In small group discussions, his group fell silent faster than the others. He was not excluded exactly—that would have been visible, actionable. He was isolated. Surrounded by people who had decided, without discussion, that he was not one of them.

I watched this happen. I told myself it would resolve on its own. New students take time to integrate. Classroom dynamics are complex. I had papers to grade and office hours to staff and a dissertation chapter due. I could not afford to involve myself in every social nuance.

One Thursday, we were discussing the scene where the creature reveals himself to the blind DeLacey. The old man welcomes him, listens to him, offers sympathy. Then the sighted family members return and drive him away. I asked: what does this scene tell us about the source of the creature's rejection?

David raised his hand. He said it tells us that people reject what they see, not what they hear. The old man responds to the creature's voice and finds him articulate, suffering, sympathetic. The family responds to his face and finds him monstrous. The rejection isn't about who the creature is—it's about what the family sees.

He was right. It was exactly right. But Emily, without raising her hand, said: "I think the point is more about expectations? Like, the family expected a monster, so that's what they saw." She was looking at me, not at David. The class's attention followed hers.

I had a choice. I could say: "That's actually what David just said. Could you respond to his point specifically?" Or I could let the redirect stand, let the discussion continue in the direction Emily had pulled it, avoid the awkwardness.

I let it stand. I nodded at Emily's rephrasing and asked a follow-up question about expectations and perception.

David closed his notebook. He didn't leave, but he stopped participating. For the rest of the semester, he sat in the circle like a shape that had been cut out of the group. He did the work. He got a B+. He never spoke again in class.

## EXTERNAL_PRESSURE

Perhaps you will say I am exaggerating, reading drama into ordinary classroom dynamics. Students have favorites and outcasts; this happens everywhere. David was not bullied, not harassed, not threatened. He was simply not accepted.

But I want you to understand what it felt like to watch. Every class meeting, fourteen pairs of eyes arranged around the circle. Thirteen of them participating in a shared conversation. One of them watching from a position that was geometrically inside the circle but socially outside it. The silence when he spoke had weight. I could feel the room's collective decision that his contributions did not count, that responding to him was not required.

I was the only person in the room with power over this dynamic. I could have named it. I could have said: "I notice that David's points aren't being engaged. Let's talk about why." I could have designed participation in ways that broke the pattern. I could have met with him privately and asked what I could do.

I felt the class's preference pressing me toward inaction. If I intervened, I would become the weird professor who made things awkward. I would lose the rapport I had built with the students who liked me. I would have to confront something I didn't fully understand and might make worse. The safe choice was to do nothing, and the room's silent consensus made doing nothing feel like neutral rather than complicit.

## THE_REALIZATION

And it was at this point, watching a room full of educated people collaborate in the soft exclusion of one of their number, that I first understood what *Frankenstein* is actually about.

The creature is not rejected because he is violent. He becomes violent because he is rejected. The rejection comes first—automatic, universal, rooted in appearance rather than behavior. Every person in the novel makes the same choice I made: to go along with the consensus that this being does not count, that his suffering is not their concern, that excluding him is not their responsibility because they are not the ones who created him.

Victor's crime is not creation. It is abandonment. But Victor is not alone in the crime. The villagers abandon the creature. The DeLacey children abandon him. William, the child, screams "monster" before he knows anything about the being he's screaming at. The abandonment is a group project. Everyone participates. No one takes individual blame.

I saw it that semester. Not in the abstract, not in a paper or a discussion question, but in the lived mechanics of a classroom. A person arrives who looks wrong, who sounds wrong, who doesn't fit the category of "people like us." The group decides, without deciding, that he doesn't count. Each individual participates in the exclusion while feeling they are not responsible for it—after all, they're just not responding to one person's comments, they're just sitting where they want to sit. But the aggregate effect is total isolation.

The novel is about this. The monster is made, not born. Every person who encounters him contributes to making him what he becomes. And every person who does so tells themselves they're not doing anything wrong.

## INTERNAL_RESISTANCE

I told myself several things during those weeks. I told myself that intervening would be paternalistic—David was an adult, capable of advocating for himself. I told myself that I might be misreading the situation, projecting my own anxieties onto ordinary classroom dynamics. I told myself that the semester was almost over, that David could take other classes, that time would solve what I could not.

But I knew what I was doing. I was choosing comfort over conscience. I was letting a student be excluded because addressing the exclusion would be awkward for me. Every time I let Emily redirect away from David's point, every time I assigned groups and didn't notice who wasn't being included, I was making a choice. The choice not to act is a choice.

I thought about this in my office, grading papers late at night. I thought about it when I saw David walking across the quad alone. I thought about it when I reread the scene where Victor destroys the half-made companion and the creature watches his only hope for connection torn apart. Victor had reasons—practical reasons, fearful reasons, reasons that felt compelling at the time. But the creature's anguish is real regardless of the reasons.

## THE_ACTION

The penultimate week of the semester, I assigned the final scene of the novel: Victor's death, the creature's last speech, his promise to destroy himself. I asked the class to read it and come prepared to discuss what the ending means—whether there is redemption, whether there is blame, whether anything could have been different.

That Thursday, I called on David first.

It was a small thing. It was nothing, really. I said: "David, we haven't heard your thoughts on this passage. What do you make of the creature's final speech?"

The room's attention swung toward him. He was silent for a moment—I thought he might decline to answer—and then he spoke. He said the creature's speech was about being seen only as a category. The creature says he expected to be hated, that he was "miserable beyond all living things." He says: "While I destroyed his hopes, I did not satisfy my own desires." He got what he wanted—Victor's attention, his fear, his obsession—and it meant nothing because the attention was still only recognition of his monstrosity, not his personhood.

He spoke for perhaps two minutes. When he finished, I looked around the circle and said: "How does this connect to what we've discussed all semester about rejection and creation?"

I called on students by name. I said: "Michael, how does David's point connect to your earlier observation about the creature's education?" I said: "Emily, you made a similar point about expectations a few weeks ago—do you see the connection?" I made them respond to him. I made them acknowledge that he had spoken and that what he said mattered.

It was awkward. The class felt the shift. A few students gave me puzzled looks. The easy rapport we'd developed all semester—the rapport that depended on letting some things slide—was suddenly different.

We finished the discussion. The semester ended. David never came to my office hours or sent a follow-up email. I don't know what, if anything, that last class meant to him. I have no reason to think it changed his experience at the college.

## CONSEQUENCE_WITNESSED

I saw David once more, the following semester, crossing the library lawn. He was alone. He was always alone when I saw him. He didn't notice me, or didn't acknowledge me if he did. He looked neither better nor worse than before—just the same large, ill-fitting figure moving through a landscape that had no place for him.

The college had done nothing wrong to David. No one had bullied him, discriminated against him, violated any policy. He had simply existed in a space that did not accept him, and the mechanisms of that non-acceptance were diffuse enough that no one was responsible for them. The monster had been made by everyone and no one.

I taught the seminar again the next year, and the year after that. I still teach it. Every time I assign *Frankenstein*, I think about David. Every time we discuss the creature's isolation, I think about the group dynamics I witnessed and participated in. The novel does not seem like a Gothic horror story to me anymore. It seems like a documentary.

## AFTERMATH_AND_FINAL_ADMISSION

Afterward, of course, I told myself that I had learned something. I had insights about the novel, about classroom dynamics, about the mechanics of soft exclusion. I could write about it (I have, here). I could use the experience in my teaching, make myself more attentive to students who are silently excluded.

And I have done those things. But I often wonder whether any of it mattered. The one thing that would have actually helped David—consistent, early, visible intervention in the dynamics that were isolating him—I did not do. I waited until the last week. I made one small gesture. Then the semester ended and I never saw him again.

I could tell you that I learned a profound lesson about complicity and action, that the experience made me a better teacher and a better person. That would be the kind of ending this story should have. But the truth is simpler and less flattering. I think about David sometimes, and I feel bad, and then I stop thinking about him because it's uncomfortable. I made one gesture at the very end because the semester was almost over and the cost of action had become lower than the cost of continued inaction.

I have often wondered whether David grasped what I did and did not do that semester. He surely noticed the exclusion; how could he not? Whether he noticed that I saw it and chose, for weeks, not to address it—whether he understood that my inaction was a choice rather than an oversight—I cannot know.

What I know is this: I let it happen because intervention would have been uncomfortable, and I intervened once at the end because continued inaction had become more uncomfortable than the alternative. I did not act from conscience. I acted from the path of least resistance, and the path happened to shift at the end of the semester.

The creature asks Victor for one thing: acknowledgment. Just see me as a person, just treat me as though my suffering matters. Victor cannot do it. Neither could I, not really, not when it would have cost me anything. I made my one gesture when the cost was lowest and the semester was ending anyway.

I did it solely to avoid feeling like a coward.
