# The Language of Monstrosity

## PROBLEM_WITH_DEFEATIST_REBUTTAL

Most people who have read *Frankenstein* would admit that the creature is mistreated. What's less acknowledged is that the mistreatment begins before the creature ever acts—before we even know what kind of being he is. The moment Victor sees his creation, he calls it "the wretch," "the daemon," "the monster." These words arrive before the creature has done anything. They are prejudgments in the literal sense: judgments that precede evidence.

Now it is generally assumed that such language is natural—that we cannot help but name things according to how they appear. A thing that looks monstrous *is* monstrous. What else would you call it? The language follows from the reality. You might as reasonably complain about calling a table a table.

But this assumption is false, or at least reversible. The language does not merely describe the creature; it creates the category that makes his treatment inevitable. "Monster" is not like "table." A table does not become more table-like when you call it one. But call something a monster often enough, and you create the conditions for it to become one. Victor's language constructs a frame that forecloses every possibility of connection. The creature does not become monstrous until after the language of monstrosity has been applied to him—and applied universally, by everyone he meets.

## REVERSIBILITY_THESIS

An effect can become a cause, reinforcing itself until it seems like the natural order. A man may call something a monster because he fears it, and then fear it more completely because he has named it monstrous. The process is reversible. If one gets rid of the monstrous framing—if one looks at the creature and asks "what is this being?" rather than announcing "this is a monster"—the entire tragic machinery of *Frankenstein* would grind to a halt.

This is not wishful thinking. Shelley provides exactly one character who does not pre-judge: the blind man, DeLacey. When the creature approaches him and speaks, DeLacey responds to the voice, not the face. He is welcoming, sympathetic, even paternal. For one moment, the frame holds differently. Then the sighted family members return, see the creature's face, and the monstrous frame reasserts itself with violence. The point is structural: the language of monstrosity is not necessary. It is a choice, and it could be chosen otherwise.

## SPECIMEN_EXHIBITION

Here are five specimens of monstrous language from the novel, which I number so I can refer back to them:

**(1)** Victor, seeing his creation for the first time: "I beheld the wretch—the miserable monster whom I had created. He held up the curtain of the bed; and his eyes, if eyes they may be called, were fixed on me."

**(2)** Victor, describing his avoidance: "I, like the arch-fiend, bore a hell within me... I was cursed by some devil, and carried about with me my eternal hell."

**(3)** The villagers' first encounter: "The whole village was roused; some fled, some attacked me, until, grievously bruised by stones and many other kinds of missile weapons, I escaped to the open country."

**(4)** Felix DeLacey, upon seeing the creature: "Who can describe their horror and consternation on beholding me? Agatha fainted; and Safie, unable to attend to her friend, rushed out of the cottage. Felix darted forward, and with supernatural force tore me from his father, on whose knees I clung."

**(5)** William Frankenstein, the child: "Let me go... monster! Ugly wretch! You wish to eat me, and tear me to pieces—You are an ogre—Let me go, or I will tell my papa."

These five passages represent not the worst examples but fairly representative ones. The language varies in intensity but not in structure. Each speaker applies the category before any hostile act by the creature. William, a child, already knows the script: monsters eat people, ogres tear things apart. The frame was taught to him before he ever encountered a being it might apply to.

## DIAGNOSTIC_CATEGORIES

I list below, with notes and examples, the varieties of monstrous language that appear in the novel. This is not a comprehensive catalog but represents the principal types.

**Reflexive Naming.** This is the first and most pervasive: the instant application of "monster," "wretch," "daemon," "fiend" without waiting to observe behavior. Specimen (1) shows Victor naming before the creature has moved or spoken. The name arrives with the visual impression as if they were the same thing. What is reflexive is that there is no gap between perception and category—no moment of uncertainty, no "what is this?" Only "monster."

The mechanism: we have pre-loaded categories for beings that look wrong. The category activates before conscious thought. The language is available, like a reflex arc, and fires without deliberation.

**Transferred Guilt.** In Specimen (2), Victor describes himself as carrying a hell, being cursed by a devil—but note how the hellish language attaches to what he *made*, not what he *did*. He does not say "I, the abandoner, am guilty." He says the creation was devilish, and he was cursed by exposure to it. The grammar transfers agency. The creature is active ("bore a hell," "was cursed by some devil") even though Victor performed every act that created the situation.

The mechanism: if the creation is demonic by nature, Victor is a victim rather than a perpetrator. The language of monstrosity exculpates the creator while condemning the created.

**Inherited Frame.** William in Specimen (5) has never seen a monster. He is a child. Yet he knows exactly what to call the creature and exactly what monsters do: eat people, tear them apart. He recites the frame he was taught. "Ogre" is a category he learned from stories. The creature, who has harmed no one at this point in the encounter, is slotted into a fairy-tale category and made to bear its implications.

The mechanism: monstrous language is culturally transmitted. Children learn the category before they ever meet a being it might apply to. When they do meet such a being, the category is waiting, pre-loaded, ready to fire.

**Physical Metaphor for Moral Status.** Throughout the novel, the creature's physical appearance is taken as evidence of moral nature. "If eyes they may be called" in Specimen (1) suggests the eyes are not quite eyes—not quite human features—and therefore the being is not quite deserving of human consideration. The DeLaceys in Specimen (4) react to sight, not speech. Felix's violence is described as having "supernatural force," as if the creature's appearance justifies supernatural response.

The mechanism: we parse moral status through physical appearance, treating ugliness as evidence of wickedness. This is not limited to fiction. It is how we treat the disfigured, the disabled, the merely unfamiliar-looking.

## PARODY_DEMONSTRATION

Let me demonstrate the effect of monstrous language by translating one of the creature's most eloquent speeches into the frame Victor applies to him.

Here is the creature, speaking to Victor in his own voice:

> "I am malicious because I am miserable. Am I not shunned and hated by all mankind? You, my creator, would tear me to pieces and triumph; shall I respect man when he contemns me? Let him live with me in the interchange of kindness, and instead of injury I would bestow every benefit upon him with tears of gratitude at his acceptance. But that cannot be; the human senses are insurmountable barriers to our union."

Now here it is in Victor's language:

> The monster raved that it would destroy unless appeased. The daemon's cunning extended to manipulation: it attempted to persuade me that its malice was my fault, that I owed it kindness. The wretch proposed we "live together"—a grotesque parody of human feeling from a thing incapable of feeling. I recognized the evil-spirited nature of its demands.

The first passage shows a being reasoning, self-aware, offering a transaction: kindness for kindness, rejection for rejection. The second passage describes the same content but makes it incomprehensible as communication. In Victor's language, the creature cannot mean what it says; its words are "cunning," its feelings are "parody," its offers are "demands." The frame has foreclosed the possibility of genuine speech. What arrives as reasoning is received as manipulation.

## MECHANISM_ANALYSIS

Why does monstrous language persist? Why do we reach for "monster" rather than "being I don't understand"?

The attraction of this way of speaking is that it is easier—even quicker, once you have the habit—to say *the monster attacked* than to say *a being whose nature and motives I have not yet assessed performed an action I found threatening*. The first version closes the question; the second opens it. Closed questions are more comfortable than open ones.

There is also the pleasure of category. Naming something "monster" places it in a familiar taxonomy: monsters are dangerous, monsters are evil, monsters are to be destroyed or fled. These implications arrive for free with the category. If the being is a monster, you already know what to do. If the being is merely strange, you must assess, consider, remain uncertain. Uncertainty is uncomfortable. Monstrous language resolves uncertainty by foreclosing assessment.

When you are frightened—which Victor always is, which the villagers are, which the DeLaceys become—the category arrives faster than thought. It is prefabricated, like the sections of a henhouse, requiring only assembly. You don't have to build a response; the response is bundled with the name.

The deeper mechanism is that monstrous language protects the speaker's self-image. If the creature is a monster, Victor is a victim of monstrosity, not a perpetrator of abandonment. If the creature is merely a person—strange-looking, unloved, desperate—then Victor is a man who abandoned his child to suffer. The language of monstrosity performs exculpation. It makes the speaker innocent by making the subject inhuman.

## POLITICAL_CONNECTION

In our time, political language is designed to make certain categories of people linguistically ineligible for sympathy. "Illegal aliens," "welfare queens," "thugs," "terrorists"—these function as monstrous language. The category arrives before the individual. The frame forecloses assessment. A "terrorist" cannot have reasons, only evil; an "illegal" cannot have a story, only a violation. The language does the work of pre-judgment that Victor's language does to the creature.

This is not merely a problem of politeness. The defense of the indefensible depends on monstrous language. If you want to separate children from parents at a border, you must first establish that the parents are "illegals," which sounds like a category of being rather than a category of documentation status. If you want to bomb a village, you must first establish that it contains "terrorists," which sounds like a category immune to civilian considerations. The creature's complaint—"I am malicious because I am miserable"—could be spoken by anyone whose treatment precedes their assessment. The language ensures they will not be heard as making an argument. They will be heard as proving their monstrosity.

What Shelley grasped in 1818 is that the construction of monsters is a social act with political consequences. The creature is not born monstrous. He is made monstrous by language that treats him as such, by people who cannot see past the language, until finally the treatment produces the behavior that justifies the treatment. The prophecy fulfills itself. And the prophets congratulate themselves on their foresight.

## SELF_IMPLICATION

Look back through this essay, and you will find that I have repeatedly used the word "creature." I chose this word deliberately because it seemed less prejudicial than "monster." But notice what it implies: a created thing, a lesser being, something that exists in relation to its creator rather than in its own right. The creature has no name in the novel. I have not given him one either. I have merely shifted from one dehumanizing term to another, less obviously pejorative.

I also called Victor a "man who abandoned his child." But the creature is not a child, and framing him that way imposes its own distortions. I have my own frames, my own pre-loaded categories. When I imagine the creature, I picture the Karloff version—flat head, bolts, green skin—despite knowing that Shelley described something quite different. The film image has colonized my imagination. The monstrous frame is in me too.

This is harder than it appears. Every attempt to describe the being in neutral terms runs into language that already carries implications. "Subject," "individual," "person"—each has connotations that shape response. There is no perfectly neutral word. But there are more and less prejudicial ones, and the direction matters even when perfection is impossible.

## RULES

I think the following rules will cover most cases:

1. Never apply a category word—monster, villain, enemy—until you have observed behavior. Appearance does not license categorization.

2. When you hear yourself using monstrous language, ask: what behavior has this being actually performed? Is the category doing work the evidence cannot support?

3. Treat categorical refusal as a warning sign. If you find yourself unable to imagine that a being could have reasons, the language is doing foreclosure work.

4. Watch for grammar that transfers agency. "The monster made me" often means "I chose to, and found it easier to blame the monster."

5. Test your frame by translation. If you describe someone's reasoning in language that makes it sound like manipulation, try translating it back to sincere speech. If the sincere version is plausible, your frame was prejudicial.

6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything that treats a suffering being as though its suffering does not matter.

## CLOSE

One cannot change this all in a moment. The language of monstrosity is ancient, culturally embedded, reflexively available. We inherited it before we could assess it. It lives in fairy tales, horror movies, political rhetoric, and the casual speech of everyday life.

But one can at least change one's own habits. And from time to time, when someone reaches for "monster" or "creature" or "thing" to describe a being they fear, one can ask: what if we just said "person"? What if we let the being speak before we named it?

The old man DeLacey, blind and therefore unable to see the creature's face, asked him to sit down and tell his story. For a few minutes, before the sighted family returned and imposed the frame, something different was possible. The creature was heard. He was answered. He was not yet a monster because no one had called him one.

You can, if you choose, be DeLacey rather than Victor. It requires attending to the habit of language—noticing when the category arrives before the evidence, when the frame forecloses the question, when "monster" does work that observation cannot justify.

And if enough people chose this often enough, some worn-out category—some "monster," "creature," "alien," "other"—might be sent into the dustbin where it belongs.
