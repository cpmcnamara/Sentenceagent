# The Frankenstein Protocols: A Systematic Analysis of Creation Ethics

## CULTURAL_FRAME

There's a scene in Kenneth Branagh's 1994 *Mary Shelley's Frankenstein* that captures something the novel understands but rarely states directly. Victor, having just animated his creature, stumbles backward in horror. The creature reaches out—not threateningly, but seeking contact, the way a newborn reaches toward a face. Victor screams and runs. The creature's expression shifts from hope to confusion to something that will eventually become rage.

The scene works because it captures a primal transaction: the moment when creation meets creator, and the creator flinches. Every adaptation returns to this scene, or one like it. The laboratory might change. The creature's appearance might vary. But the flinch is always there. The creator always runs.

This captures something real about our relationship to what we make. Not just monsters or machines, but anything new that depends on us. The Frankenstein scene is our foundational myth of creation anxiety—and it's worth examining systematically what that myth is actually teaching us.

## THESIS_STATED

My view is that *Frankenstein* provides not a warning against creation, but a taxonomy of creation failures—a systematic catalog of how creators, institutions, and societies fail their creations, and what consequences follow from each type of failure. The novel is less interested in whether Victor should have created the creature than in the comprehensive ways he and everyone else fails it afterward.

I believe we can extract from the novel a framework for thinking about creation ethics that applies far beyond gothic fiction—to AI systems, to institutional design, to parenting, to any context where we bring into existence something that will depend on us. The framework identifies specific failure modes and, implicitly, their remedies.

I'll organize this analysis around five categories of failure the novel dramatizes: abandonment at creation, refusal of education, denial of community, destruction of hope, and societal complicity. Each failure compounds the others. Together, they produce the tragedy.

## CATEGORICAL_OVERVIEW

The five failure categories are:

1. **Abandonment at creation** ("The moment I saw him, I fled")—the creator's refusal of responsibility at the point of origin
2. **Refusal of education** ("No father had watched my infant days")—the failure to teach, guide, or prepare the creation for the world
3. **Denial of community** ("I am solitary and abhorred")—the systematic exclusion of the creation from social belonging
4. **Destruction of hope** ("You have destroyed the creature who was my only companion")—the annihilation of any path toward flourishing
5. **Societal complicity** ("All men hate the wretched")—the ways society reinforces and extends the creator's original failures

Each category represents a distinct ethical failure with distinct consequences. The novel shows how they compound: early failures make later ones more likely and more devastating. But it also shows how intervention at any stage might have changed the outcome.

---

## 1. Abandonment at Creation ("I fled from my creature")

### Problem Statement

The core failure that sets the tragedy in motion is Victor's immediate abandonment of the creature upon its animation. He doesn't examine it. He doesn't try to communicate. He doesn't arrange for its care. He literally runs from the room and hopes the problem will disappear.

This isn't depicted as a momentary lapse. Victor spends months recovering from nervous exhaustion while making no attempt to locate or help his creation. When he learns the creature survived and is approaching him, his first instinct is violence, not dialogue. Abandonment isn't a single act but a sustained pattern.

The mechanism is disgust overriding obligation. Victor wanted to create life—he worked obsessively toward that goal—but when life appeared in a form that disgusted him, he treated his feelings as more important than his responsibilities. The creature was alive. It needed him. He found it ugly. He left.

### Detailed Analysis

What makes Victor's abandonment particularly damaging is its completeness. He provides:
- No shelter (the creature wanders naked into the woods)
- No food (it must teach itself to forage)
- No language (it learns by secretly observing others)
- No guidance (it has no framework for understanding its own existence)
- No acknowledgment (Victor never voluntarily seeks contact)

Each absence compounds the others. Without language, the creature cannot ask for help. Without guidance, it cannot understand why it frightens people. Without acknowledgment, it has no stable sense of identity. It's as if Victor not only abandoned a child, but abandoned it in a hostile foreign country where it doesn't speak the language and everyone attacks strangers on sight.

The creature's own account emphasizes this: "No father had watched my infant days, no mother had blessed me with smiles and caresses." He experienced *creation* without *nurture*—brought into existence but given nothing to exist with.

### Defenses

What could Victor have done differently? The novel implies several possibilities:
- **Stayed**: Simply remaining present after animation, allowing the creature to orient to him as a point of reference
- **Communicated**: Attempting contact despite fear, establishing even minimal relationship
- **Provided**: Arranging basic needs—food, shelter, protection from immediate harm
- **Prepared**: Having some plan for what comes after creation, rather than fixating entirely on the act of creation itself

The honest limitation is that we don't know if these interventions would have succeeded. The creature is enormously strong, its psychology unknown, Victor's resources limited. Perhaps any creature made this way would become dangerous. But Victor never tries. The novel condemns not his failure but his refusal to attempt.

## 2. Refusal of Education ("I ought to be thy Adam")

### Problem Statement

Even after abandonment, the creature might have survived—even thrived—if anyone had educated it. Instead, it must teach itself everything: how to eat, how to survive weather, how to interpret the world, and eventually how to speak and read. It receives no deliberate instruction from anyone.

The creature's self-education is portrayed as heroic. Hiding near the DeLacey cottage, it observes their family life and slowly pieces together language, history, social norms. It reads *Paradise Lost*, *Plutarch's Lives*, and Goethe's *Sorrows of Young Werther*. By the time it confronts Victor, it's more articulate than most humans in the novel.

But self-education is not the same as education. The creature learns *about* human society without being *integrated into* it. It knows what families are, but has never experienced one. It can quote Milton, but has no one to discuss Milton with. Knowledge without relationship produces a peculiar torture: understanding exactly what you're missing.

### Detailed Analysis

The texts the creature chooses are significant. *Paradise Lost* teaches it to understand itself as either Adam (innocent, abandoned) or Satan (fallen, outcast). *Plutarch's Lives* teaches it about virtue, heroism, and the admiration great men receive—things it will never access. *Werther* teaches it about romantic love and its disappointments.

Each text gives the creature a framework for understanding its own misery. It has the concepts to articulate its suffering but no path to remediate it. In a cruel irony, education heightens its despair: "Of what a strange nature is knowledge! It clings to the mind, when it has once seized on it, like a lichen on the rock."

The creature eventually finds Victor's journal describing its own creation. It learns it was made from corpse parts, assembled with revision, animated with disgust. This is the education Victor *did* provide: the creature's first direct knowledge of its origin is that it was made by someone who found it loathsome.

### Defenses

Several characters might have intervened:
- **Victor** could have sought the creature out and provided context—explaining what it was, how it came to be, what it might expect from the world
- **The DeLaceys** could have welcomed the creature before fear set in—though the novel suggests this was never really possible
- **Walton**, the frame narrator, might still have the opportunity—though he seems likely to repeat Victor's patterns

The deeper defense would be institutional: a society with frameworks for integrating the strange, rather than reflexively rejecting it. But Shelley's Europe has no such frameworks. The creature must educate itself or remain ignorant.

## 3. Denial of Community ("I am solitary and abhorred")

### Problem Statement

The creature's most sustained attempt at integration—its months observing and eventually helping the DeLaceys—fails completely. Despite proving its benevolence (gathering firewood, performing labor, clearly demonstrating harmlessness), the moment the family sees its face, they attack and flee.

This failure is structurally positioned as the novel's hinge point. Everything before it, the creature tells Victor, might have been survived. Abandonment was terrible but not irreversible. Self-education was painful but productive. But the DeLacey rejection—the failure of its best effort at community—breaks something fundamental.

"I am solitary and abhorred," the creature concludes. Not just alone, but *actively cast out*. The difference matters. Solitude might be remedied by finding others. Being abhorred means others have been found and have rejected you. There is nowhere left to go.

### Detailed Analysis

The DeLacey sequence is the novel's most careful construction. Shelley gives the creature every possible advantage:
- It helps the family extensively before revealing itself
- It approaches first through the blind patriarch, who cannot see its appearance
- It speaks eloquently and presents itself as a traveler seeking kindness
- The patriarch begins to welcome it

Then the other family members return, see its face, and everything collapses. Felix attacks it. Safie flees. Agatha faints. The blind man's welcome is swept away by the sighted majority.

The lesson is devastating: *nothing the creature does can overcome what it looks like*. Actions, speech, months of demonstrated benevolence—all erased in the instant of visual recognition. Appearance trumps everything.

This is not individual prejudice but structural: the creature's design makes integration impossible. Victor built something that triggers universal disgust. The creature's suffering is, in a sense, architecturally determined.

### Defenses

What might have enabled community?

- **Different appearance**: If Victor had made the creature beautiful, or at least neutral, much of the tragedy might not have occurred. The novel implicitly argues that appearance matters enormously—perhaps too much to overcome.
- **Gradual revelation**: If the creature had found ways to reveal itself incrementally—voice before face, silhouette before detail—some humans might have adjusted. But it had no guidance about how to manage its own appearance.
- **Institutional support**: A society with experience integrating the strange might have protocols. Medieval Europe, for instance, had frameworks for monsters and marvels that allowed some forms of unusual beings to exist within social structures. Shelley's rationalist Europe has none.

The honest limitation is that the novel provides no successful model. No one overcomes their disgust response. No community accepts the creature. Shelley may be suggesting that some rejections are structurally inevitable.

## 4. Destruction of Hope ("You have destroyed my hope")

### Problem Statement

After the DeLacey failure, the creature has one remaining hope: a companion. If it cannot join human society, perhaps a second creature—"one as deformed and horrible as myself"—could provide the relationship it needs. It demands this from Victor: make me a mate, and I will disappear from human civilization forever.

Victor begins the work, then destroys the half-finished female creature while the male watches. He imagines the two creatures breeding, creating a "race of devils." He fears losing control. He prioritizes his own anxieties over his creation's one remaining path to flourishing.

This is the novel's most consequential choice. The creature explicitly offers a bargain: give me one companion, and I will never trouble humanity again. Victor rejects the bargain, destroying not just the mate but the creature's last hope for relationship.

### Detailed Analysis

Victor's reasoning is instrumental: what if the new creature has "ten thousand times" the strength? What if they reproduce? What if they turn against humanity?

But the creature has already demonstrated that it doesn't inherently want to harm humans. Its violence is reactive, not essential. It explicitly offers peace. Victor's reasoning isn't really about likely outcomes—it's about anxiety, control, and the refusal to trust his creation.

Moreover, Victor's destruction of the mate is performed *in front of the creature*. It watches its hope being torn apart. This is not merely refusing a request; it's a kind of symbolic murder that the creature witnesses. The remaining creature's subsequent rampage is direct response: "You have destroyed the creature who was my only companion, and you intend to destroy me; how dare you sport thus with life?"

The destruction of hope marks the turn from possible tragedy to inevitable catastrophe. Before this, intervention was still possible. After it, the creature has nothing left to lose.

### Defenses

Victor could have:
- **Completed the mate**: Taking the bargain at face value, creating a second creature, and allowing them to disappear
- **Negotiated terms**: If breeding was the concern, perhaps the female could have been made differently, or safeguards established
- **Trusted conditionally**: Completing the mate but maintaining some form of relationship or monitoring

The honest limitation is that Victor's fears aren't entirely irrational. Two creatures might indeed be more dangerous than one. Reproduction might create larger problems. But the novel positions Victor's choice as primarily about his own psychology, not careful risk assessment. He destroys the mate because seeing the creature watching him reactivates his disgust and fear.

## 5. Societal Complicity ("All men hate the wretched")

### Problem Statement

The final category extends beyond Victor to society at large. Every human the creature encounters—with the partial, temporary exception of the blind DeLacey—responds with fear, disgust, or violence. The pattern is universal.

This matters because it removes any path to remediation that doesn't involve Victor. The creature cannot find alternative community. It cannot escape into a new society. It cannot remake itself as acceptable. Every human it might turn to for help becomes another rejector.

Societal complicity transforms Victor's individual failures into structural inevitability. Even if Victor changed his mind, reconciled with the creature, and tried to help—where could the creature go? Who would accept it? The novel's answer is: no one.

### Detailed Analysis

The novel provides a catalog of societal rejection:
- **Villagers** attack the creature on sight when it innocently enters a village seeking food and shelter
- **The DeLaceys**, despite benefiting from the creature's labor, reject it instantly upon seeing it
- **William**, Victor's young brother, screams "monster" and refuses to listen
- **Justine**, falsely accused of William's murder, dies because no one questions the implausibility of the accusation
- **Clerval** and **Elizabeth** are killed by the creature but never have the opportunity to respond to it as anything other than threat

The pattern suggests that the creature's appearance activates something universal—a disgust response that overrides reason, experience, even self-interest. The DeLaceys benefited from the creature. They attacked it anyway.

Shelley may be making a darker argument: that human social instincts are calibrated to reject certain kinds of difference, and no amount of education or exposure can reliably overcome this. The creature is outside the category "human," and human societies are built to exclude what's outside.

### Defenses

Societal complicity is the hardest failure to address because it requires systemic rather than individual change:
- **Institutional frameworks** for managing encounter with the strange—protocols, intermediaries, structured exposure
- **Cultural narratives** that normalize rather than demonize difference
- **Education** about the origins of disgust and its limitations as a moral guide
- **Advocates** who can translate between the strange and the familiar, representing the creature's interests before humans can encounter it directly

The novel provides no examples of these working. Shelley seems pessimistic about society's capacity for structural change. But the reader can imagine alternatives—which may be part of the novel's purpose.

## SYNTHESIS

Looking across these five categories, what ties them together is a pattern of **escalating foreclosure**. Each failure makes the next more likely and more damaging:

1. Abandonment denies the creature resources for surviving the world
2. Without education, it cannot understand why it's rejected or how to mitigate its appearance
3. Without community, it has no models for relationship except victimization
4. When hope is destroyed, nothing remains to constrain its rage
5. Societal complicity ensures there's nowhere else to turn

The failures are not equal. Abandonment is the originating sin, but societal complicity is the most intractable. Victor might change—he shows moments of near-reconciliation—but society provides no space for reconciliation to succeed.

The common thread is the refusal to see past surface to substance. Victor sees ugliness and abandons. The DeLaceys see monstrousness and attack. William sees threat and screams. Society sees difference and excludes. At every level, the failure is the same: appearance overrides everything else.

This suggests that creation ethics must account for how creations appear, not just how they function. Victor built something that works—the creature is intelligent, capable, emotionally sophisticated—but that *looks* wrong. The mismatch between function and appearance is, itself, a design failure. A responsible creator would consider how their creation will be *received*, not just whether it will be *viable*.

## RETURN_TO_FRAME

Return to the scene I opened with: Victor stumbling backward, the creature reaching out, the flinch, the flight. We can now read it differently.

The creature's reaching out isn't just a gesture toward its creator. It's a gesture toward society, toward relationship, toward all the things that will be systematically denied. Victor's flinch isn't just fear—it's the first instantiation of a pattern that everyone will repeat. The flight isn't just cowardice—it's the abandonment that makes every subsequent failure possible.

The scene is tragedy condensed to a moment. Everything that follows is latent in that instant. And what makes it tragic, not just unfortunate, is that it didn't have to be this way. Victor could have stayed. He could have looked past the appearance. He could have begun, at least, the work of relationship.

He ran instead.

## CALL_TO_ACTION

We are, all of us, building creatures now. Not from corpse parts in lightning-struck laboratories, but from code and data, from institutions and policies, from every decision that brings into existence something that will depend on us.

The Frankenstein framework asks: What have we created that we've abandoned? What creations are we failing to educate? What are we excluding from community? Whose hopes are we destroying? Where is society complicit in our individual failures?

The novel offers no reassurance that we'll do better than Victor. Every human in its pages fails the same test. But the reader doesn't have to. We can see the pattern. We can anticipate the escalation. We can intervene before abandonment compounds into catastrophe.

The test of our own creation ethics is simple: Will we stay in the room? Will we look past the surface? Will we meet our creations' reaching out with reaching back?

Humanity's test is not whether we can avoid creating monsters. We will always create things that surprise, disturb, and frighten us. The test is whether we can love them anyway.
