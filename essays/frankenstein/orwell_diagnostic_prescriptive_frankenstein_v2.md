# The Language of Monstrosity (Revised)

## PROBLEM_WITH_DEFEATIST_REBUTTAL

Most people who read *Frankenstein* would admit that the creature is mistreated—but it is generally assumed that such mistreatment is natural, that we cannot help but react with horror to what horrifies us. The language follows from reality. A thing that looks monstrous *is* monstrous; what else would you call it?

This assumption is false. The language does not describe; it constructs. Call something a monster often enough and you create the conditions for it to become one. Victor's "wretch" and "daemon" arrive before the creature has moved or spoken. The category precedes the evidence, and once applied, forecloses every possibility of connection.

## REVERSIBILITY_THESIS

An effect can become a cause, reinforcing the original and producing the same result in intensified form. A child may fear dogs because one barked at him, and then have more dogs bark at him because his fear makes them anxious—until he believes all dogs are aggressive, though his own fear creates the aggression he fears. The process is reversible. Change the initial response, and the reinforcing cycle breaks.

So too with monstrous language. Victor's terror produces the flight that produces the creature's isolation that produces the violence that seems to justify the terror. If one gets rid of the monstrous frame—if one asks "what is this being?" rather than announcing "this is a monster"—the entire tragic machinery of the novel would grind to a halt. Shelley proves this: the blind DeLacey, who responds to voice rather than appearance, welcomes the creature as a fellow sufferer. The frame can hold differently. It is a choice.

## SPECIMEN_EXHIBITION

Here are five specimens of monstrous language, which I number so I can refer back to them. They are not especially bad examples—they are fairly representative of how the novel's characters speak:

**(1)** Victor, seeing his creation for the first time:

> "I beheld the wretch—the miserable monster whom I had created. He held up the curtain of the bed; and his eyes, if eyes they may be called, were fixed on me. His jaws opened, and he muttered some inarticulate sounds, while a grin wrinkled his cheeks. He might have spoken, but I did not hear; I escaped, and rushed down stairs."

Note "wretch" and "monster" in the first sentence, before any threatening act. "If eyes they may be called"—the creature's features are denied human status. "He might have spoken"—Victor flees rather than hear what the being might say.

**(2)** The villagers' first encounter (reported by the creature):

> "I had hardly placed my foot within the door, before the children shrieked, and one of the women fainted. The whole village was roused; some fled, some attacked me, until, grievously bruised by stones and many other kinds of missile weapons, I escaped to the open country, and fearfully took refuge in a low hovel."

The reaction is immediate and unanimous. "Shrieked," "fainted," "fled," "attacked"—four verbs of rejection in one sentence. The creature has done nothing but appear.

**(3)** Felix DeLacey's response to seeing the creature with his blind father:

> "Who can describe their horror and consternation on beholding me? Agatha fainted; and Safie, unable to attend to her friend, rushed out of the cottage. Felix darted forward, and with supernatural force tore me from his father, on whose knees I clung: in a transport of fury, he dashed me to the ground, and struck me violently with a stick."

"Supernatural force"—even the violence is amplified by the monstrous frame. The creature, who has spent months secretly helping this family, is beaten with a stick for the crime of being seen.

**(4)** William Frankenstein, a child:

> "Let me go! monster! Ugly wretch! You wish to eat me, and tear me to pieces—You are an ogre—Let me go, or I will tell my papa."

A child's language reveals what he has been taught. "Monster," "ogre," "eat me," "tear me to pieces"—William already knows the script. He has learned from fairy tales what monsters do. The frame was in place before he ever encountered a being it might apply to.

**(5)** Victor, after years of pursuit:

> "All that I say may appear strange to you; but you will easily perceive that nothing can be more hateful to me than the daemon who has brought me to this state; I reserve the vengeance which is his due."

"Daemon" is not description but classification. It places the creature in a category—supernatural evil—that justifies "vengeance" rather than inquiry. The language forecloses the possibility that Victor might be culpable for what the "daemon" has become.

## DIAGNOSTIC_CATEGORIES

I list below, with notes and examples, the varieties of monstrous language that appear in these specimens. This is not exhaustive but represents the principal types.

**Verbal Flinches.** The instant, reflexive application of "monster," "wretch," "daemon," "fiend" before any threatening behavior. In Specimen (1), Victor names before the creature has moved or spoken. The name arrives with the visual impression as if they were inseparable. There is no gap between perception and category—no moment of "what is this?" Only "monster."

*Mechanism*: We have pre-loaded categories for beings that look wrong. The category fires before thought, like pulling back from a hot stove. The language is available—ready-made, prefabricated, requiring only the reflex of fear to activate it.

**Grammar of Innocence.** The grammatical structures that transfer agency from the speaker to the creature. In Specimen (5), Victor says the "daemon" has "brought me to this state"—passive voice hiding his own acts. In Specimen (1), "I escaped" makes Victor the acted-upon party rather than the actor who chose to flee. The creature is active; Victor merely responds.

*Mechanism*: If the creature is demonic by nature, Victor is a victim of demonic visitation rather than a perpetrator of abandonment. The grammar exculpates by making the speaker innocent of choice.

**Inherited Scripts.** Language learned from stories and applied before experience. William in Specimen (4) has never seen a monster, but he knows exactly what monsters do: eat people, tear them apart. These are fairy-tale actions, not observed behaviors. "Ogre" is a category from nursery tales, ready to be applied to any frightening face.

*Mechanism*: Monstrous language is culturally transmitted. Children learn the category before they could possibly meet a being it applies to. When they do meet such a being, the category is waiting, loaded, ready to fire.

**Denied Features.** The description of a being's physical attributes as not-quite-real, not-quite-belonging. In Specimen (1), "if eyes they may be called" denies the creature's eyes the status of eyes. In Specimen (3), Felix's strength becomes "supernatural"—as if contact with the creature transforms ordinary violence into something mythic.

*Mechanism*: If the creature's features are not quite features, the creature is not quite a being. Denying physical reality denies moral status. The technique turns a person into a thing by treating his body as not quite a body.

## PARODY_DEMONSTRATION

Let me demonstrate the effect of monstrous language by translating a passage every reader recognizes and loves.

Here is Jesus, in Matthew 25, speaking of the Last Judgment:

> "I was a stranger and you took me in: Naked, and you clothed me: I was sick, and you visited me: I was in prison, and you came unto me... Verily I say unto you, Inasmuch as you have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, you have done it unto me."

Now here it is in the monstrous language of the novel:

> "I was a stranger—a wretch, a thing of horror—and you fled from me. Naked, and you turned your eyes from my deformity. I was sick, and you did not come unto me, for who can visit such a daemon? I was in prison—the prison of my own hideous flesh—and no man came, for they said: It is not truly a creature; if eyes they may be called, they are not eyes we can meet. Verily I say unto you, inasmuch as you have failed to see one of the least of these my brethren—inasmuch as you have named him monster and fled—you have done it unto me."

The first version is about universal dignity. The second forecloses it. In the monstrous frame, the stranger cannot be received because the language has already categorized him as something that cannot be received. The command "take me in" becomes impossible when the hearer has already named the speaker "wretch."

## MECHANISM_ANALYSIS

Why does monstrous language persist? Why do we reach for "monster" rather than "being I do not yet understand"?

The attraction of this way of speaking is that it is easier—even quicker, once you have the habit—to say *the monster attacked* than to say *a being whose nature and motives I have not yet assessed performed an action that frightened me*. The first version closes the question. The second opens it. Closed questions require no further work; open questions demand attention, consideration, the possibility of being wrong.

There is the pleasure of category, clicking into place like a bullet into a chamber. Once "monster" is loaded, you know what to do: flee, fight, warn others. If the being is merely strange, you must remain uncertain, and uncertainty sits in the mind like an unswallowed pill, irritating the throat.

When you are frightened—which Victor always is, which the villagers are, which the DeLaceys become—the category arrives faster than thought. It is prefabricated, like flat-pack furniture, requiring only the Allen key of terror to assemble. You do not build a response from raw materials; the response is bundled with the name, shrink-wrapped, ready for use.

The deeper mechanism is self-protection. If the creature is a monster, Victor is a victim of monstrosity rather than a perpetrator of abandonment. The language works like a faulty fuse box, tripping the circuit before any current of responsibility can flow through. It is safer to be attacked by a daemon than to have created a child you failed.

## POLITICAL_CONNECTION

In our time, this same language is used to make certain categories of people linguistically ineligible for sympathy.

A man arrives at a border with his family. He has walked for months. He carries his child because the child is sick. He asks for help:

This is called *illegal entry*. The children are *separated*—as if they had been naturally adjacent and are now merely being *returned to proper positions*. The father is *detained*—which sounds like being kept for a meeting rather than locked in a cage. If he dies in detention, it is an *adverse medical event*.

A man in another country has beliefs we find dangerous. His village is *cleared*—not burned, though it burns; not its inhabitants killed, though they die. The strike is *surgical*, as if the bombs were scalpels and the rubble a kind of healing. Civilian deaths are *collateral damage*, which sounds like something broken during shipping—regrettable but incidental, the cost of doing business.

A woman is hungry. She receives *assistance*—not food, which is concrete and must come from somewhere, but *assistance*, which sounds like it comes from a help desk. When the assistance is cut, she has been *transitioned*—from the rolls, from the count, from the category of people we are obligated to notice.

The creature's complaint—"I am malicious because I am miserable"—could be spoken by anyone whose treatment precedes their assessment. But the language ensures they will not be heard as making an argument. "Illegal," "detained," "transitioned"—these words do the work of "monster." They foreclose the possibility of sympathy before the case can be heard.

## SELF_IMPLICATION

Look back through this essay, and you will find that I have repeatedly used the word "creature." I chose it because it seemed less prejudicial than "monster." But notice what "creature" implies: a created thing, a lesser being, something that exists in relation to its maker rather than in its own right.

The being in the novel has no name. Shelley never gave him one; neither have I. Every term available—creature, being, monster, wretch—carries implications I cannot escape. I have slid from category to category throughout this essay, trying to find neutral ground that does not exist.

I also find, looking back at my parody of Matthew, that I put "daemon" in the mouth of my imagined speakers—as if the word were obviously wrong. But I use "daemon" as a quotation of the novel, without always noting that it is the novel's frame, not reality. The monstrous language has infected my own text in ways I only half-notice.

## RULES

I think the following rules will cover most cases:

1. Never apply "monster" or equivalent before observing behavior.
2. When you hear monstrous language, ask what behavior justifies it.
3. Watch for grammar that transfers agency from speaker to target.
4. Distrust inherited categories—especially from childhood stories.
5. Test your frame by translating to neutral language: does the case still hold?
6. Break any of these rules sooner than refuse sympathy to a suffering being.

## CLOSE

One cannot change this all in a moment. The language of monstrosity is ancient, embedded in fairy tales and horror films and political rhetoric and the casual speech of everyday fear. We inherited it before we could assess it.

But one can at least change one's own habits. And from time to time, when someone reaches for the ready-made category—some "monster," "creature," "alien," "illegal," "other," "thing," "thug," "savage," "beast," "inhuman"—one can ask: what has this being actually done? What would we call them if we did not already know what to call them?

The blind man DeLacey, who could not see the creature's face, asked him to sit down and tell his story. For a few minutes, before the sighted family returned and the flinching category was reimposed, something different was possible. The creature was heard. He was not yet a monster because no one had called him one.

You can, if you choose, be DeLacey rather than Victor. It requires attending to your language—noticing when the category arrives before the evidence, when the grammar shifts blame, when the word "monster" does work that observation cannot justify.

And if enough people noticed often enough, perhaps some worn-out and useless phrase—some "illegal," "terrorist," "thug," "savage," "monster," "beast," "alien," or other lump of verbal refuse—might be sent into the dustbin where it belongs.
