# The Wrong Monster

## QUESTION

My goal here is to figure out why *Frankenstein* endures. Not just survives—plenty of novels survive by inertia—but actively *endures*, the way certain stories keep getting adapted and reimagined and argued about for two centuries. Despite its title, the novel isn't really about a doctor named Frankenstein. Or maybe it is, but not in the way we usually think. What would it mean to understand what this novel is actually doing?

The question matters because we've gotten something systematically wrong about it. When people invoke "Frankenstein" as a metaphor, they almost always mean the same thing: science gone wrong, the hubris of creation, the danger of playing God. But I'm not convinced that's what the novel is about at all.

## FIRST_ATTEMPT

The obvious interpretation is that *Frankenstein* is a cautionary tale about scientific overreach. Victor Frankenstein wants to create life, succeeds, and the result is catastrophic. Prometheus stealing fire. Icarus flying too high. Man shouldn't play God.

This reading isn't wrong exactly. It's in the novel's subtitle: *The Modern Prometheus*. Mary Shelley clearly intended some kind of warning about ambition. And generations of Hollywood adaptations have reinforced this—the mad scientist in his laboratory, the monster breaking free, the torches and pitchforks of the frightened villagers.

But you can say this about almost any story of creation gone wrong. What makes *Frankenstein* special? If it were merely "don't create things you can't control," we'd have forgotten it by 1850. The Prometheus myth already covered that ground.

What seems worth investigating is why the creature, not the creation act, is what we remember. The thing we call "Frankenstein" in popular culture isn't Victor. It's the monster. We got the names confused, and the confusion might be telling us something.

That may sound obvious, but it has some unexpected consequences.

## UNEXPECTED_CONSEQUENCE

One is that the creature is the novel's moral center, not its villain. When you actually read the book—not watch the films, not absorb the cultural shorthand—something strange happens. The creature is eloquent. He reads Milton's *Paradise Lost*. He helps a poor family in the woods. He begs for a companion, for love, for acknowledgment. Victor is the one who behaves monstrously: he abandons his creation at birth, refuses responsibility, prioritizes his own reputation over everyone's safety.

Another unexpected consequence: the creature's violence is almost entirely reactive. He doesn't kill because he's evil. He kills because he's been rejected by every human he meets, including and especially his creator. Each murder is preceded by an attempt at connection that fails. He's not Godzilla, mindlessly destroying. He's a child who was never taught anything, punished for existing.

I imagined when I started that I'd be writing about why we fear creation. But in fact the opposite seems true. What *Frankenstein* actually shows is why *abandonment* is the real monster. Victor's sin isn't that he created life. It's that he refused to parent it.

## WRONG_TURN_ACKNOWLEDGED

If that's true, then I started with the wrong question entirely. Asking "why is creation dangerous?" was leading me away from the novel, not toward it. The Prometheus frame—fire stolen, gods angered—was a distraction.

Mary Shelley wasn't warning against scientific ambition. She was 18 when she wrote the first draft. She'd lost her first baby just a few months before. The novel is riddled with childbirth imagery, abandonment trauma, failed families. Her mother died giving birth to her. Her father grew cold and distant. Creation wasn't abstract to Shelley—it was intimate, terrifying, and deeply personal.

Perhaps what this shows is that *Frankenstein* isn't about the dangers of science at all. It's about what happens when we refuse to love what we've made.

## BETTER_QUESTION

Instead of asking "why is creation dangerous?", I should have asked: **why does everyone fail the creature?**

Not just Victor—though he fails spectacularly—but everyone. The DeLacey family, who the creature loves and helps, drive him away when they see his face. The villagers attack him on sight. William, Victor's little brother, screams at the sight of him rather than listen. Every human in the novel fails the same test: can you love something that looks wrong?

There is, and on the face of it, it seems almost identical to the original question. But the shift matters. The first framing puts creation at the center—Victor's choice to build, the Promethean overreach. The second puts *response* at the center—what happens after creation, how society treats the new and strange.

How do you build a creature everyone will reject?

## MECHANISM

Here's how it works. Shelley constructs the creature to be maximally sympathetic on the inside and maximally monstrous on the outside. He's intelligent, articulate, capable of love, desperate for connection. But his appearance is so horrifying that no one can see past it.

The gap between surface and depth is the engine of the novel's tragedy. Every encounter follows the same pattern: the creature approaches with hope, the human sees his face, the human reacts with disgust or violence, the creature retreats into despair. Repeat until the despair curdles into rage.

What makes this mechanism so powerful is that readers fall for it too. When you start the novel, you expect the creature to be the villain—that's what the cultural frame has taught you. As you read, you undergo the same reversal the DeLaceys should have: you learn to see past the surface. You find yourself sympathizing with the "monster" and condemning the "hero."

Shelley is teaching you, by experience, what the characters fail to learn. The mechanism of reading mirrors the mechanism of the plot.

I don't consciously notice this on first reading. I just find myself increasingly frustrated with Victor's cowardice, increasingly moved by the creature's isolation. But what's happening underneath is a systematic deconstruction of my expectations. Every chapter peels away another layer of assumption.

The creature's central speech to Victor—spanning several chapters in the novel's middle—is structured to maximize this effect. He tells his story chronologically: awakening, confusion, hiding, observing the DeLaceys, learning language, learning love, attempting contact, rejection, fury. By the time he's done, any reader with a heart understands that this is not a monster but a person. And the "hero" who made and abandoned him looks increasingly pathetic.

## IMPLICATIONS_FOLLOWED

If this is true—if the novel is fundamentally about failed love rather than dangerous creation—then several other things follow.

The first is that the "mad scientist" reading isn't just incomplete, it's backwards. Victor's crime isn't ambition. It's *abandonment*. The creation itself is ethically neutral. What matters is what comes after: the refusal to teach, nurture, and accept. "I ought to be thy Adam," the creature says, "but I am rather the fallen angel." He recognizes his own innocence, and recognizes that his fall was imposed from outside.

The second is that the creature's violence, while terrible, is comprehensible. It's not random or malicious. It's the predictable outcome of a child raised with no love, no guidance, no mirror of humanity. Every murderous act is preceded by failed reaching-out. The creature kills William only after William screams in terror and refuses to listen. He kills Clerval and Elizabeth to make Victor feel what he feels: the loss of everyone who might have loved him.

This suggests the novel might be a psychological study before its time. Shelley is showing how monsters are made, not born. The creature begins innocent—fascinated by birdsong, moved to tears by the DeLaceys' poverty, desperate for any scrap of affection. Society creates the monster by refusing to see past his surface.

A third implication: the novel indicts not just Victor but civilization itself. Every human the creature meets fails the same test. The DeLaceys, poor and marginalized themselves, still can't extend empathy to something uglier than them. Even the blind old man, DeLacey senior, who initially welcomes the creature's voice, is swept away when the others return. The rejection is unanimous.

If everyone fails, the problem isn't individual—it's structural. Shelley is saying something about what humans do to the strange, the ugly, the unfamiliar. We other them. We make monsters by treating them as monsters.

## OBJECTION_ADDRESSED

You might think I'm over-reading. Shelley was 18, writing a ghost story for a contest. Isn't it simpler to say she just wanted to scare people, and the depths I'm finding are accidents?

I'd concede that not every element of the novel is consciously designed. First novels rarely are. But the central structure—the creature's eloquent autobiography nested inside Victor's increasingly pathetic self-justifications, nested inside Walton's framing narrative—is too deliberate to be accidental. The narrative architecture ensures we hear the creature's side of the story. We get his voice, in his own words, for dozens of pages. That's not an accident.

Moreover, the biographical evidence matters. Shelley's mother died giving birth to her. Her first baby died in infancy. She knew, intimately, the terror of creation and the anguish of loss. The novel is saturated with maternal imagery: Victor "gives birth" alone in his laboratory, then flees in horror from what he's made. The creature compares himself to Adam—not Lucifer, but Adam, innocent and abandoned. These aren't horror movie props. They're the materials of lived trauma, processed into fiction.

Perhaps the deepest objection is that I'm blaming victims: the DeLaceys, the villagers, even little William. Aren't they entitled to fear something that looks terrifying?

But that's precisely Shelley's point. *Of course* they're entitled to fear. The creature is hideous. The reaction is natural. And it's still wrong. Being natural doesn't make it just. The novel is asking whether we're capable of anything better than our natural reactions—whether we can choose love over fear, curiosity over disgust. The answer, in the novel, is no. But the reader is invited to do better.

## CLOSE

So the answer to why *Frankenstein* endures is not that it warns against creation. Plenty of creation myths warn against creation, and they're footnotes.

*Frankenstein* endures because it asks a question we still haven't answered: what do we owe to the things we make? Not just to children, though that's the obvious echo. But to AI, to immigrants, to anyone who looks wrong or sounds wrong or shows up uninvited. The creature is monstrous, eloquent, dangerous, innocent, murderous, sympathetic—all at once, irresolvably. That's why we keep adapting him. Every generation sees their own pariahs in his face.

I understand better now where the confusion about the title comes from. We call the creature "Frankenstein" because, on some level, we know he matters more than his creator. Victor made something and walked away. The creature asked to be loved and was refused. Which of them is really the monster?

The novel doesn't answer that question. It makes us feel it.
