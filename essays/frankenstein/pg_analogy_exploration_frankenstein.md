# Founders and Monsters

## SURPRISING_PAIRING

When I finished reading *Frankenstein* for the third time, I kept thinking about startup founders. This seemed strange. The novel is 200 years old, written by a teenager in a Swiss villa, about a scientist who builds a monster from corpse parts. What could it possibly have to do with people building apps in San Francisco?

A lot of people seemed surprised when I mentioned the connection. They seemed to think gothic horror and venture capital were very different domains. One friend laughed and said "Victor Frankenstein wasn't trying to make money." Another pointed out that YC doesn't fund reanimation projects.

Both of these images are wrong. They miss the structural similarity underneath.

## CORE_SIMILARITY

What Victor Frankenstein and startup founders have in common is that they're both *makers who create things that escape their control*. They're both in the business of bringing into existence something new, something that will take on a life of its own, something that will eventually act according to its own logic rather than its creator's intentions.

Along with novelists, parents, scientists, and anyone else who makes things that persist beyond the making moment, founders and Victor belong to a category we might call *consequential creators*. They don't just make artifacts—they make *entities*, things that continue to exist and act after the creator steps back.

This is different from making a chair or writing a memo. Those are objects. A startup, like Frankenstein's creature, is a system that grows, adapts, interacts with its environment, and often becomes something its creator never intended.

## CONVENTIONAL_FRAME_CRITIQUE

I've never liked the phrase "build something people want." Not because it's wrong, but because it treats startups as products rather than entities. It puts all the emphasis on the making moment—what you build—and almost none on what happens after.

The main reason I don't like it is that startup failure modes map eerily onto Victor's failures, and very few of them are about building the wrong thing. Look at how startups actually die:

- Founder abandonment (they get tired, move on, lose interest—Victor's first sin)
- Failure to adapt to the creation's actual nature (Victor expected gratitude; founders expect users to behave as planned)
- Refusal to address problems once they emerge (Victor knew the creature was dangerous and did nothing)
- Cutting off the creation's path to success out of fear (Victor destroying the mate; founders pivoting away from what's working because it scares them)
- Society rejecting what's been built, forcing it into adversarial postures (the creature becoming monstrous because it was treated as a monster)

At one end of the startup spectrum, you have founders who stay with their creations for decades—Bezos, Zuckerberg, the Collison brothers. In the middle, you have founders who exit after getting the company to a stable state. At the other extreme, you have founders who abandon ship at the first sign of trouble, or who never really commit to their creation in the first place.

The most dangerous founder is the one who's brilliant at building but terrible at nurturing. Victor Frankenstein, basically.

## LESSONS_FROM_ANALOGY

What can Frankenstein teach us about founding? Several things, I think.

**Lesson 1: Creation is the beginning, not the end.**

Victor's fundamental error is treating animation as the finish line. He works obsessively for months, barely eating, barely sleeping, entirely focused on the question of whether he *can* bring the creature to life. He never once asks what happens after.

I was taught something similar in business school: focus on the launch. Hit your milestones. Get to product-market fit. Everything after that is "execution," which is implicitly less important, something you can hire people to do.

But Victor had product-market fit. The creature worked. It was intelligent, capable, adaptive—vastly exceeding any reasonable specification. And the whole thing still fell apart, because Victor hadn't thought about what came next.

For a long time I felt bad about startups that struggled after launch, as if there was something wrong with founders who found the post-launch phase hard. Now I realize the problem is structural: we train people to obsess over building and give them almost no preparation for what follows. The creature emerges, the founder looks at it in horror, and there's no playbook for staying in the room.

**Lesson 2: Your creation will not match your vision, and that's the test.**

Victor expected gratitude. He imagined the creature looking up at him with reverence, recognizing its creator, offering thanks. What he got was something that looked wrong, that reached toward him, that had needs he hadn't anticipated.

Founders expect the same thing. They imagine users adopting the product for the intended use case, behaving as the spec predicted, generating the metrics that were forecast. What they get is a creature: something that uses the product unexpectedly, demands features that weren't planned, generates consequences that weren't modeled.

Here's a case where we can learn from Victor's failure. The creature's reaching toward Victor was not a threat—it was an attempt at connection. It became a threat only after Victor's rejection forced it to be. Similarly, users doing unexpected things with a product aren't necessarily a problem; they're information about what the creation has become. The founders who succeed are the ones who can look at what they've actually made, rather than what they intended to make.

**Lesson 3: Abandonment is the unforgivable sin.**

Victor's abandonment cascades through the entire novel. Because he wasn't there at the beginning, the creature had no foundation. Because it had no foundation, it couldn't integrate with society. Because it couldn't integrate, it became desperate. Because it was desperate, it became dangerous.

The equivalent for founders is what we might call "ghost founding"—raising money, building an MVP, hiring a team, and then disengaging. The founder stays on paper but stops paying attention. The creature is left to fend for itself in a hostile world.

I've watched startups die this way. Not from lack of funding or lack of product-market fit, but from lack of care. The founder stopped showing up emotionally, stopped adapting to what the company needed, stopped being present. The company became monstrous—not deliberately malicious, but acting in ways the founder never intended and would have hated—because nobody was steering anymore.

Victor's creature became a murderer. Abandoned startups become something similar: value-destroying entities that harm employees, customers, and markets. The creature in the novel kills four people. A badly-abandoned startup can damage thousands.

**Lesson 4: Denying your creation's needs guarantees failure.**

The creature's request for a mate is reasonable. It doesn't ask to be unmade. It doesn't demand to live among humans. It asks only for one companion, promising to disappear forever afterward.

Victor refuses. His stated reason is fear of reproduction, of losing control, of a "race of devils." But the real reason, visible in the text, is that he can't stand the thought of his creation thriving on its own terms. Better to destroy it than let it succeed in ways he didn't plan.

Founders do this constantly. A startup finds a use case the founder didn't intend—maybe less prestigious, maybe less aligned with the original vision—and the founder pivots away from it. They'd rather kill the company than let it become something they didn't design.

This is Victor destroying the mate. The creation was working. It was finding its own path. And the founder, out of ego or fear or simple distaste, destroys the path rather than following it.

## INSTITUTIONAL_FAILURE

If universities and VCs actually understood what founding entails, they wouldn't train founders almost exclusively in building. The building is the easy part—not easy in absolute terms, but easy compared to what comes after.

Unfortunately, most institutions don't recognize this. Y Combinator talks about building things people want. Venture capital evaluates pitches and prototypes. Business schools teach strategy and finance. Nobody teaches what Victor needed to know: how to stay with your creation when it looks wrong, when it demands things you didn't expect, when it becomes something you didn't intend.

I only discovered this myself by watching founders struggle. The ones who failed weren't less brilliant at building—often they were more brilliant. They failed because they couldn't handle the creature.

The place to look for counterexamples is founders who've done it multiple times. They know something. Watch how they behave after launch: the attention to what's actually happening rather than what was planned, the willingness to adapt, the sustained presence. They've learned what Victor never learned—that the creation is not the achievement, but the beginning of responsibility.

## WAY_FORWARD

So one way to think about founding is as a commitment to stay in the room. Not just to build, but to watch what emerges, to respond to what it becomes, to adapt as it grows.

This is what parenting requires. It's what art requires—you don't stop engaging with a novel because the first draft is done. It's what Victor refuses to provide.

Practically, this means:

- Plan for the post-launch period as intensively as you plan for building
- Expect your creation to surprise you, and treat surprises as data rather than threats
- Stay present even when—especially when—the creation looks wrong
- Let the creation become what it's becoming, rather than forcing your original vision
- Recognize that abandonment cascades, that early failures compound into later catastrophes

The startup ecosystem is getting better at some of this. There's more focus on founder mental health, more acknowledgment that the journey is long, more support for the post-launch period. But we're still far from recognizing the core truth: that founding is a form of parenting, and the creature requires care for as long as it exists.

## CLOSE_WITH_STAKES

What we can say with some confidence is that the Frankenstein pattern keeps recurring. Every new technology creates new creatures—entities that escape their creators' control, that develop their own logic, that sometimes become monstrous.

The internet was a creature. Social media was a creature. AI will be a creature. Each time, the founders and builders focus on the creation question—can we make this work?—and underestimate the nurture question—what do we do when it works?

Over and over we see the same pattern: brilliant building, inadequate stewardship, creatures that escape and cause harm. Victor Frankenstein is not a historical oddity. He's a prototype. And we keep replicating him.

How cool our creations turn out to be will depend on whether we can break the pattern. Not by refusing to create—creation will happen regardless—but by staying with what we make. Looking at it when it's ugly. Adapting to what it becomes. Taking responsibility not just for the animation moment, but for everything after.

Victor ran from the room. The question for founders, and for all of us who make consequential things, is whether we can stay.
