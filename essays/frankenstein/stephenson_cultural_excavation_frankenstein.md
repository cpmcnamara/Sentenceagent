# The Monster's Face: An Excavation

## HISTORICAL_FRAMING

About two hundred years ago, in the summer of 1816, a group of English literary expatriates found themselves trapped by weather in a villa on Lake Geneva. Lord Byron had rented the Villa Diodati. Percy Bysshe Shelley and his future wife Mary were staying nearby with Mary's stepsister Claire Clairmont, who was pregnant with Byron's child. It was the Year Without a Summer—volcanic ash from Mount Tambora had dimmed the sun across the northern hemisphere, and rain fell incessantly.

Byron proposed a contest: everyone would write a ghost story. He and Percy Shelley started pieces they soon abandoned. John Polidori, Byron's physician, wrote "The Vampyre," which would later inspire Bram Stoker. And Mary Shelley, eighteen years old, recently bereaved of her first child, began *Frankenstein*.

The business took off quickly. By 1818, the novel was published. By 1823, a stage adaptation was touring London. By the 1930s, Universal Studios had turned the story into its most iconic horror franchise. A person who went into a coma in 1816 and woke up now would find that a teenage girl's nightmare had become a permanent fixture of global culture—referenced, adapted, parodied, invoked as shorthand for technological anxiety in contexts Mary Shelley couldn't have imagined.

What happened to the story along the way is as interesting as the story itself.

## EXTENDED_METAPHOR_SETUP

The analogy between Mary Shelley and Victor Frankenstein is not half bad, so let me run with it for a moment.

Mary created something from dead parts—bits of Prometheus myth, chunks of Paradise Lost, scraps of contemporary galvanic theory, fragments of her own grief. She stitched them together in isolation, labored over the animation, and sent the result into the world.

The creature promptly escaped her control.

The novel she wrote is about abandonment, grief, failed love, and the systematic destruction of an innocent by a society that refuses to see past surfaces. But the cultural creature called "Frankenstein"—the one that shambles through movie screens and Halloween costumes and political rhetoric—is about something else entirely. It's about hubris, about science gone wrong, about the danger of playing God.

Mary's creature was eloquent, philosophical, heartbroken. The cultural creature is mute, violent, stupid. Mary's creature read Milton. The cultural creature grunts and breaks things. Mary's creature was the novel's moral center; the cultural creature is a monster in the simplest sense—something to fear and destroy.

Imagine a crossroads where four roads meet. On one road comes Mary Shelley's creature: articulate, reasoning, tragic. On another comes James Whale's creature: Boris Karloff with bolts in his neck, reaching toward a little girl and accidentally drowning her. On a third comes the creature of popular culture: the green face, the flat head, the Halloween mask. On the fourth comes the rhetorical creature: the invocation of "Frankenstein" in headlines about GMOs and AI and anything else that makes us nervous about technological change.

These are not the same creature. They share a name and a general origin story—scientist creates artificial life, things go wrong—but they differ in nearly every particular. Standing at the crossroads, watching them approach, you would not know they were related.

What the hell is going on here?

## CULTURAL_ARCHAEOLOGY

The first thing to understand is that the novel was almost immediately adapted, and adaptation is a form of mutation. Richard Brinsley Peake's 1823 stage play, *Presumption; or, The Fate of Frankenstein*, already transforms the creature: it's mute, its complexity is flattened, its violence is foregrounded. The play was successful enough to spawn imitations, and each imitation drifted further from the source.

This is not such a bad thing in some ways—it's how culture propagates. But it means that most people's knowledge of Frankenstein has always been secondhand. They know the adaptation of the adaptation of the adaptation. They know the version that traveled best, which is the simplest version: mad scientist, dangerous creation, torch-bearing villagers.

The metaphor shear was especially brutal across media. On stage, you couldn't have the creature deliver dozens of pages of eloquent autobiography—audiences wouldn't sit still for it. In film, the creature became a visual spectacle whose appearance was more important than anything it might say. Boris Karloff's performance was brilliant, but it was a performance of pathos and menace, not articulation.

By the time the creature reached me—through Universal movies on late-night TV, through parodies and Halloween costumes, through the cultural shorthand of "Franken-" as a prefix meaning "horrifically artificial"—it had lost almost everything that made it interesting in the novel. I knew there was a book. I assumed the book was like the movie, only less cinematic.

I was in my late twenties before I actually read it. The dissonance was so extreme it felt like encountering a different story entirely.

(Note: many readers might wonder where this is going. Rest assured that I am actually positioning my pieces on the chessboard. The excavation has to proceed layer by layer. We are currently in 1931, with Boris Karloff, and we need to go deeper.)

Here's what I mean by "deeper." The transformation of the creature from eloquent innocent to grunting monster wasn't random. It served a function.

The novel is, among other things, an indictment of society. Every human in the book fails the creature—not just Victor, but the DeLaceys, the villagers, little William, everyone. The creature's monstrosity is manufactured by human cruelty. If you read the novel seriously, you come away disturbed not by the monster but by the humans.

The adaptations reverse this. The creature is innately dangerous. The scientist is reckless but not malevolent. Society is right to fear the monster, right to grab torches and pitchforks, right to drive it into the windmill and burn it alive. The victim becomes the villain; the indictment becomes an endorsement.

This reversal makes the story easier to consume. A monster you can blame is simpler than a monster society created. A mad scientist you can shake your head at is simpler than a creator whose sins are ordinary neglect. The adapted Frankenstein lets us off the hook. The original Frankenstein does not.

I myself had an experience that illustrates this. When teaching the novel in a university course, I asked students to predict what would happen after the creature's creation. They assumed it would immediately attack Victor—that was what monsters did. When instead the creature reached out pathetically and Victor fled, they were confused. The cultural template was so strong that the actual text felt wrong.

The cultural creature is not a corruption of the original; it's a defense against it. We prefer the version that makes us innocent.

## PHILOSOPHICAL_DIGRESSION

The word "monster" comes from the Latin *monstrum*, meaning "omen" or "portent"—something sent by the gods to warn or instruct. Monsters were not originally defined by their malevolence but by their function as signs. A two-headed calf was a monster not because it was dangerous but because it indicated something.

In this older sense, Mary Shelley's creature is indeed a monster: it signifies, it portends, it teaches. What it teaches is uncomfortable—that we manufacture the things we fear, that our rejection creates the threats we then use to justify further rejection—but it teaches.

The cultural creature has lost this semiotic function. It's a monster in the degraded sense: something scary. It means nothing beyond its threat. The green face and flat head communicate only "be afraid." There's no instruction, no omen, no sign.

We want things to be easier. A monster that simply is monstrous requires no interpretation. A monster that became monstrous through our own actions requires us to examine ourselves. We chose the first, over and over, across two centuries of adaptation.

This is precisely the same as what happens to most complex ideas when they enter popular culture. Nuance erodes. Complexity flattens. The version that survives is the version that requires the least from its audience. Simplicity has a selection advantage in cultural transmission; complexity is a handicap.

The word, in the end, is the only system of encoding thoughts that preserves complexity reliably. Images are too immediate; performances are too fleeting; the culture of adaptation and reference is too prone to decay. Mary Shelley wrote her monster in words because words are the only medium that can capture internal experience—what it feels like to be rejected, to learn, to hope, to despair.

The creature's autobiography, in the novel's center, is the irreducible thing that cannot survive adaptation. You cannot film internal experience. You cannot stage it in a way that fits a theatrical rhythm. The creature's interiority—its reading of Paradise Lost, its identification with Adam and Satan, its loving observation of the DeLaceys—can only exist as prose.

When the adaptations stripped away the creature's words, they stripped away its humanity. What remained was the body: the bolted neck, the shambling gait, the reaching hands. The body without the mind is, indeed, monstrous. The adaptations created the monster they then depicted.

We are implicated in this. Every time I put on a Frankenstein mask for Halloween, every time I used "Frankenword" to describe a technological creation that disturbed me, I was participating in the erasure of Mary Shelley's creature. I was choosing the simple monster over the complex one.

## RESOLUTION_OR_IMPASSE

The only real question is whether the original can survive alongside its copies. Is there room in culture for both the eloquent creature and the grunting one? Can we hold simultaneously the novel's indictment and the movies' reassurance?

In the end, there's just a fundamental tension between what we want from our monsters and what Mary Shelley offered. We want monsters to be monstrous—to justify our fear, to externalize our violence, to give us something clearly threatening so we can feel clearly threatened. Shelley offered a mirror instead: look at what you do to the strange and vulnerable, and see who the real monster is.

She lost, in a sense. The cultural creature won. The mirror was replaced by a mask. The indictment was softened into entertainment.

But the novel survives. Every few years, someone reads it for the first time and experiences the vertigo I experienced—the encounter with a creature who is nothing like the cultural icon, who speaks in sentences we might have written ourselves, who asks only for love and is answered with violence.

What would the creature say, if it could see what we've made of it? Perhaps what it said to Victor, after Victor destroyed its mate: "You have denied me my companion, and I am alone."

We have made the creature alone in a different sense—alone in meaning what it means, surrounded by hollow copies that use its face but not its voice. And still it speaks, to anyone who reads the words Mary Shelley wrote down in that rainy summer. Still it makes its case. Still it asks to be seen.

The only way to hear it is to close the browser, turn off the screen, open the book, and let an eighteen-year-old's ghost story excavate something in us we'd rather not find.
