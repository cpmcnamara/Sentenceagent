# How to Not Be Victor Frankenstein

## MISCONCEPTION_STATED

The way to avoid creating a monster is not to think harder before you create.

That sounds backwards. Conventional wisdom says disasters come from insufficient foresight—more planning, more analysis, more anticipation of consequences. Victor Frankenstein's problem, in this reading, is that he didn't think through the implications of reanimating the dead.

But that's not what Shelley shows us. Victor thought constantly. The problem wasn't in his planning phase. It was in every moment after his creation opened its eyes—when he could have engaged with what he'd made and instead chose to flee. Not insufficient forethought. Insufficient afterthought.

## BETTER_MODEL

Here's the model that actually works: creation requires commitment, and commitment means staying present for consequences.

Victor treated creation as a one-way transaction. He put in work, he got a result, he was done. But creation isn't a transaction—it's the beginning of a relationship. The creature wasn't a product; it was an entity that would make demands, have needs, and respond to how those needs were met.

The better model separates three things that Victor conflated:
- **Capability**: Can I make this? (Victor's obsession)
- **Evaluation**: Now that I've made it, what is it actually? (Victor's failure)
- **Stewardship**: Given what it is, what does it need from me? (Victor's abandonment)

Most creators fixate on capability. They want to know if they can do the thing. But capability is the easy part. The hard parts are evaluation—honestly assessing what you've created—and stewardship—committing to what your creation needs.

Victor had capability in abundance. He built a living being from dead tissue. But his evaluation was instant and emotional: "I had selected his features as beautiful... but now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart." He looked once, judged, and fled.

Stewardship never happened at all. The creature's first experiences were abandonment, confusion, fear. When it finally found its creator, Victor's response was horror and threats. The creature didn't become a monster because Victor made it. It became a monster because Victor refused to care for it.

## FILTER_CRITERIA

How do you know if you're doing this right? Here are the filters:

**The evaluation test**: Can you describe your creation without using the words you hoped to describe it with? Victor wanted to create something beautiful and glorious. When it wasn't, he couldn't describe it as anything other than hideous. If you can only see your creation through the lens of your expectations, you're not evaluating—you're projecting.

**The needs test**: Do you know what your creation requires? Not what you want to give it. What it actually needs. The creature needed guidance, language, social integration, purpose. Victor never asked. He assumed—wrongly—that bringing something to life ended his obligation.

**The response test**: When your creation does something unexpected, do you adapt or resist? The creature's appearance was unexpected. Its intelligence was unexpected. Its capacity for language and moral reasoning was unexpected. Victor treated each surprise as an offense rather than information.

**The ownership test**: Would you tell people about your creation? Victor kept his work secret—before, during, and after. Secrecy is usually a sign that you're avoiding accountability. If you can't explain what you've made to someone else, you probably haven't reckoned with it yourself.

## HOW_TO_NOTICE

The skill here is noticing when you're slipping into Victor mode. Your mind has strong incentives to flee from complicated creations, and it's good at disguising the flight.

One technique: notice when you feel revulsion toward something you made. Revulsion is often disappointed expectation dressed up as moral judgment. Victor felt "breathless horror" at the creature, but that horror was really about the gap between what he'd imagined and what he'd built. The creation wasn't wrong; his expectations were.

Another technique: notice when you're avoiding the artifact itself. Victor spent months after the creation's escape not looking for it, not investigating, not asking questions. He busied himself with other things. If you find yourself working on everything except your creation, that's a sign you're fleeing.

A third technique: notice when you're explaining your creation to yourself. The creature needed no explanation. Victor's elaborate justifications—it's hideous, it's unnatural, it was a mistake—were self-soothing. If you're spending more time explaining why you shouldn't engage with your creation than actually engaging, you're Victor.

## WELLS_IDENTIFIED

Where do you look to find opportunities for better stewardship? Frankenstein suggests several fertile areas:

**The moment of completion.** Victor's critical failure came the instant the creature opened its eyes. This is when you're most vulnerable to flight—the work is done, the adrenaline is fading, and suddenly you have to deal with a thing instead of a process. Schedule explicit time for post-completion engagement. Don't let success become the end.

**The first unexpected behavior.** When the creature climbed through Victor's window, Victor saw threat. He could have seen communication. The first time your creation does something you didn't anticipate, you have a choice: treat it as information or treat it as malfunction. Choose information.

**The first request.** The creature's first coherent request to Victor was to be heard—to tell his story. Victor reluctantly agreed, and it's the closest he comes to actual engagement. When your creation asks something of you, that's the stewardship opportunity. Don't deflect.

**The moment of doubt.** When others questioned Victor—his father, Elizabeth, Clerval—he deflected and dissembled. But doubt from others is a gift. They see what you're invested in not seeing. When someone expresses concern about your creation, lean in rather than defending.

## RECIPES

Here's a concrete process for not being Victor:

**Recipe 1: The first-hour protocol.** When your creation is finished, spend the first hour with it. Not celebrating, not sharing it—just observing. What does it actually do? How does it differ from what you expected? Write down ten observations about what it is, not what you hoped it would be.

**Recipe 2: The needs inventory.** Make a list of everything your creation might need from you. Include obvious things (maintenance, resources) and non-obvious things (explanation, defense, adaptation). For each need, ask: am I willing to provide this? If not, why not?

**Recipe 3: The outsider test.** Describe your creation to someone who wasn't involved in making it. Don't justify or explain—just describe. Watch their reaction. Where they express concern or confusion, you have blind spots. Victor never did this. He kept the creation entirely private, which meant he never got outside perspective.

**Recipe 4: The abandonment audit.** Once a week, ask yourself: what have I avoided dealing with about this creation? Write it down. Then pick one item and engage with it directly. Avoidance compounds. Small flights become large ones.

**Recipe 5: The revision commitment.** When you discover something wrong with your creation, fix it rather than hide it. Victor saw flaws in the creature and decided to destroy his second creation rather than improve his process. Destruction is not stewardship. Revision is.

## WARNING_SIGNS

You're doing it wrong if:

**You're telling the creation's story for it.** Victor speaks for the creature constantly—explaining what it is, what it wants, why it acts. But the creature is eloquent. It can speak for itself. If you're narrating your creation rather than letting it demonstrate what it is, you're controlling rather than stewarding.

**You're hoping it will go away.** Victor's fantasy is that the creature will leave him alone. He promises to create a companion if the creature will just disappear. If you're negotiating with your creation to minimize your involvement, you've already abandoned it.

**You're blaming the creation for being what it is.** Victor resents the creature for being hideous, for being demanding, for existing. But the creature didn't choose its form. Victor did. If you're angry at your creation for its properties, you're angry at yourself and displacing it.

**You're escalating rather than engaging.** Victor moves from avoidance to rejection to active hostility to attempted murder. At no point does he try conversation, compromise, or collaboration. If your relationship with your creation is getting more adversarial over time, you're Victor.

## CLOSE_PRACTICAL

The creature makes one request that contains all of this: "Do your duty towards me, and I will do mine towards you and the rest of mankind." That's the recipe in one sentence. Not enthusiasm, not pride, not even love—just the willingness to show up for what you've made.

Start there. Pick one thing you've made that you've been avoiding. Go back to it. Look at it honestly. Ask what it needs. Do one thing for it. Victor's tragedy isn't that he created something dangerous—it's that he refused to stay. Whatever you're building, you can choose differently.
