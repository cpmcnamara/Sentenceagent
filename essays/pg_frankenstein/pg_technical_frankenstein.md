# The Isolation Thesis

## CREDENTIALS_ESTABLISHED

I've taught Frankenstein to engineering students for ten years, always as a cautionary tale about hubris. Then I actually counted the words. "Hubris" appears zero times. "Overreach"—zero. But "solitary" appears dozens of times. "Secret" even more. We've been teaching the wrong lesson for two hundred years.

## CONTROVERSIAL_CLAIM

Here's my thesis: Frankenstein isn't about the dangers of creation. It's about the dangers of creating in isolation.

Victor's experiment isn't condemned because it was ambitious. It's condemned because he did it alone, in secret, without telling anyone, without seeking help, and—crucially—without anyone able to see what he was doing and say "stop" or "think harder" or "what happens next?"

The creature isn't a punishment for hubris. It's a consequence of isolation. And the entire tragedy of the novel could have been prevented if Victor had worked with others instead of against them.

## SKEPTIC_ANTICIPATED

The obvious objection: surely the novel is about the hubris of creating life. Victor explicitly compares himself to God. The subtitle is "The Modern Prometheus." Isn't that clearly about overreach?

Look at what actually happens. Victor isn't punished for creating life—he creates life and then abandons it. The creature becomes destructive only after being rejected by its creator and society. If Victor had stayed, taught the creature, introduced it to others, the outcome would have been completely different. The creature itself says this explicitly: "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend."

Besides, Shelley was surrounded by people doing ambitious, transgressive things. Her mother wrote proto-feminist philosophy, her father radical political theory, her husband poetry celebrating Prometheus. Mary herself conceived Frankenstein at Villa Diodati in a group setting—Byron's famous ghost-story challenge. The Shelley circle believed in collective creation and mutual critique. Victor represents the opposite: the creator who refuses collaboration.

## CONCEPTUAL_TOOL

I want to introduce a concept I call the "isolation trap."

The isolation trap is the belief that important work requires working alone. It comes in two forms:

**Heroic isolation**: The idea that great achievements come from solitary geniuses. Victor embraces this fully. "I shunned my fellow creatures as if I had been guilty of a crime," he says of his working period. He sees isolation as necessary for greatness—he couldn't let others distract him or steal his glory.

**Protective isolation**: The idea that risky work should be hidden from others who wouldn't understand. Victor hides his experiment because "I knew well... that if any other had communicated such a relation to me, I should have looked upon it as the ravings of insanity." He assumes others would stop him, so he doesn't give them the chance.

Both forms lead to the same trap: without outside perspective, you lose the ability to evaluate your own work. Victor can't see the creature clearly because he has no one to compare perceptions with. When it's hideous to him, he has no way to know if that's his exhaustion, his disappointed expectations, or an accurate assessment.

The isolation trap also disables your ability to course-correct. When things go wrong, you have no resources to draw on. Victor faces the creature alone, runs alone, deals with consequences alone. Each decision compounds his isolation because he can't tell anyone about the previous decisions.

Shelley understood that creation is social, even when the work is technical. Ideas need friction. Ambition needs constraint. Capability needs accountability. Remove those, and you get Victor—a brilliant man who builds something he can't understand, can't evaluate, and can't manage.

## MECHANISM_EXPLAINED

Why does isolation corrupt creation? The mechanism has three parts.

First, **isolation removes feedback loops**. When Victor works with no observers, he has no way to know if he's going wrong. He describes his work as obsessive, feverish, unhealthy—but he only sees this clearly in retrospect. In the moment, isolation feels like focus. Without someone to say "you've been in that laboratory for six months without seeing sunlight," obsession looks like dedication.

Second, **isolation prevents reality-testing**. Victor's expectations of the creature are wildly off. He expects beauty; he gets horror. He expects gratitude; he gets need. If he'd described his project to anyone, they could have asked: "What do you think it will want? How will it feel about being the only one of its kind? What happens when it sees your reaction to it?" Victor never answers these questions because no one asks them.

Third, **isolation escalates commitment**. When you've worked on something alone for years, admitting failure means admitting you wasted that time alone. Victor can't tell anyone the creature exists because then he'd have to explain why he didn't tell them earlier. Secrets breed secrets. Isolation locks in.

The creature understands this better than Victor. When the creature makes its case, it essentially argues: "You made me in isolation, raised me in isolation, and left me to isolation. Is it any wonder I became what isolation makes people?" The creature's education happens in hiding—watching a family through a crack in a wall. It learns language and morals, but it learns them without practice, without real relationship. Even the creature's learning is isolated, and Shelley makes clear this is why it goes wrong.

## EVIDENCE_STACKED

Consider the evidence that Shelley is writing about isolation, not creation.

**Textual evidence**: Count the references to secrecy and isolation in Frankenstein. Victor hides his work from his family, his professors, his friend Clerval. The creature hides from humanity. Walton, the narrator, is isolated at the North Pole. The word "solitary" appears dozens of times. The word "secret" appears even more. Meanwhile, "hubris"? Zero. "Overreach"? Zero.

**Structural evidence**: Every major tragedy in the novel follows from isolation. Victor works alone; he creates something he can't manage. The creature lives alone; it becomes bitter and violent. Victor keeps the creature secret; the murders go unexplained and his family dies. Even Walton's frame narrative is about the dangers of isolation—he's warned explicitly that his polar expedition risks "the inestimable benefit of a companion."

**Character evidence**: The happy characters in Frankenstein are the connected ones. The DeLacey family, whom the creature watches, are impoverished but content because they have each other. Clerval is joyful because he's engaged with others. Elizabeth is serene because she has community. The isolated characters—Victor, the creature, Walton at the start—are miserable.

**Biographical evidence**: Shelley knew isolation. Her mother died eleven days after giving birth to her. She eloped at sixteen, was estranged from her family, lost children, and spent years in the unstable orbit of Percy Shelley's chaos. But she also knew collaboration—the Villa Diodati summer that produced Frankenstein was intensely social, creative, argumentative. She'd experienced both isolation and its opposite. The novel is a testimony to what she learned.

**Genre evidence**: Frankenstein wasn't received as a warning against science. Contemporary reviewers focused on its emotions, its relationships, its commentary on society. The "hubris" reading is largely a twentieth-century imposition, especially from film adaptations. Shelley's novel is less "mad scientist" than "lonely man."

## COMPETITIVE_FRAME

Why does this matter? Because the isolation thesis has practical implications the hubris thesis doesn't.

If Frankenstein is about hubris, the lesson is: be humble, don't overreach, respect limits. But that's nearly useless advice. Limits aren't clear in advance. Humility is often wrong—sometimes you should believe you can do something unprecedented. The hubris reading offers nothing except caution, which frequently translates to cowardice.

The isolation thesis offers something better: work with others. Show your work. Build in feedback loops. Find people who will tell you when you're wrong. These are actionable recommendations. They apply whether you're building a creature, a company, or a piece of software.

The competitive frame is this: people who read Frankenstein correctly will collaborate more effectively than people who read it as a warning against ambition. The ambitious collaborators will outcompete the humble loners and the isolated geniuses alike. Victor's actual failure mode—working in secret, refusing feedback, fleeing from consequences—is everywhere in technology, politics, and business. The people who recognize it have an advantage.

## CLOSE_PROVOCATIVE

Here's a test: when Victor describes working alone, does it seem romantic or tragic? When the creature asks for a companion, does it seem like a threat or a solution? Shelley's answers are clear. Isolation is tragic. A companion might have been the answer—Victor destroys it because he's unable to imagine sharing his creation with anyone.

Two hundred years of "don't play God" have taught us the wrong lesson. The right lesson is: don't play God alone. Creation isn't the sin. Solitude is.
