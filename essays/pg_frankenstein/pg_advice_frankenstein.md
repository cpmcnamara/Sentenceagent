# What Victor Frankenstein Didn't Know

## SITUATION_SETUP

A few years ago I gave a talk to some computer science students about starting ambitious projects. Afterward, during questions, someone asked what I wished I'd known at their age. I told them to read Frankenstein.

Not the movie version—the one in Mary Shelley's novel. The one where a brilliant young scientist spends years obsessively building something, succeeds beyond his wildest expectations, and then immediately runs away from it. I keep seeing that pattern in smart, ambitious people—including, embarrassingly, myself. They're so focused on whether they can do something that they never think about what happens the morning after they succeed.

What would have helped Victor? What would help the rest of us?

## EXPECTED_ADVICE_REJECTED

The standard advice about ambitious projects is some variant of "think before you act." Plan carefully. Consider the consequences. Know what you're getting into.

This sounds wise but it's almost useless. First, because you can't actually know what you're getting into. The whole point of doing something ambitious is that it hasn't been done before. Victor couldn't have predicted exactly how his creature would turn out because nobody had ever reanimated dead tissue. The unknowns are the definition of the territory.

But more importantly, "think before you act" addresses the wrong phase of the project. Victor did think. He thought obsessively for years. He planned, researched, experimented. The disaster didn't come from insufficient forethought. It came from what happened in the moment of success—and every moment after.

## REFRAMING_PREMISE

The real question isn't how to avoid consequences. It's how to stay present for them.

Victor's actual failure wasn't creating the creature. It was that the instant his creation opened its eyes, Victor fled. He didn't examine it, speak to it, or take any responsibility for what he'd made. He ran to his bedroom, had nightmares, and woke to find the creature had wandered off. Then he spent months pretending nothing had happened.

This is the part nobody warns you about. Success in ambitious projects creates a new situation that demands your immediate attention. And your instinct, especially if the result is more complicated or disturbing than you expected, will be to avoid dealing with it. Victor didn't fail in the laboratory. He failed in the moment he should have said "okay, this is different than I imagined, now what?"

## MECHANISM_REVEALED

Here's why this happens: ambitious projects are usually motivated by the desire to prove something—to yourself or others. Victor wanted to show he could conquer death. But proving you can do something is backward-looking. It's about validating a past version of yourself. The moment you succeed, you've answered that question. And suddenly you're faced with an entirely different one: what do I do with this thing I've made?

The psychological shift is jarring. You go from hunter to caretaker overnight. It's like the difference between courting and marriage—the skills that win someone over are not the skills that make a partnership work. Obsessive focus, willingness to sacrifice everything, the ability to ignore doubts: these are exactly wrong for what comes next. Caring for a creation requires presence, not obsession. It requires responding to what is, not what you imagined.

There's a reason Victor describes the creature as hideous the moment it comes to life. It probably wasn't objectively worse than the body parts he'd been stitching together for months. What changed was that he was finally looking at it. When you're building something, you see the potential. When it's done, you see the reality. The gap can be devastating.

This is also why so many founders disappear after their startups succeed. They wanted to build a company; they didn't want to run one. The building was the validation they sought. The running is just work—and work for which they're often badly suited.

The creature itself understood this better than Victor. "I ought to be thy Adam," he tells his creator later, "but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed." The creature didn't fail Victor. Victor failed the creature—not by making it, but by refusing to engage with it once it existed.

## CONTRAST_SERIES

Think about the difference between starting and continuing. Starting a project is exciting. You're unconstrained, full of possibility, answerable only to your vision. Continuing means dealing with what you've actually built—its limitations, its needs, its demands on your time and attention. Victor wanted the glory of starting without the burden of continuing.

Or consider the difference between creating and parenting. Creation is an act of will and imagination. Parenting is response and adaptation. You can create according to your own timeline, but once something exists, its needs don't wait for you to be ready. The creature needed guidance immediately. Victor wasn't interested in guidance. He was interested in the experiment.

There's also a distinction between ambition and responsibility. Ambition is about what you want to achieve. Responsibility is about what you owe to your achievements. They feel like they should be connected, but they run on different psychological tracks. Victor had enormous ambition and almost no sense of responsibility. He wanted to be the person who conquered death; he didn't want to be the person who raised the conqueror.

Finally, notice the difference between private and public consequences. Victor's project was done in secret. He told no one, asked for no help, kept his work entirely private. But the creation couldn't stay private. It entered the world. It affected others. Victor acted as if he could contain both the work and its effects within the sphere of his own ambition. That's not how consequences work.

## ACTIONABLE_ADVICE

So what do you actually do?

First, assume that success will feel nothing like you expect. The accomplishment you've imagined for years will arrive as a stranger. Plan for estrangement. When your project works, schedule time immediately—not to celebrate, but to sit with it. Examine what you've made. Notice how it's different from what you imagined. Don't run.

Second, separate the question "should I have done this?" from "what do I do now?" Victor tortured himself with the first question when he should have been answering the second. Regret is a luxury. Responsibility is a practice. Even if you've made something you wish you hadn't, that thing exists. Your feelings about having made it don't change what it needs from you.

Third, build support structures before you need them. Victor worked in total isolation, which meant when things got complicated, he had no one to help him think. Find people who can look at your creation without your emotional investment. They'll see things you can't. And when you want to flee, they can remind you not to.

Fourth, expect your creation to have its own trajectory. The creature wasn't what Victor imagined, and it didn't do what Victor would have wanted. But Victor had no right to expect otherwise. Once something exists, it has its own logic. Your job is to respond to what it actually is, not to insist it become what you planned.

## OBJECTION_ANTICIPATED

The obvious objection is: some projects really shouldn't be finished. Maybe Victor should have destroyed the creature, or never made it at all. Maybe running was the right response.

But that's not what Victor did. He didn't make a decision—he made an escape. He didn't destroy the creature; he didn't engage with it; he didn't seek help. He fled and hoped the problem would disappear. The creature didn't become murderous because Victor made it. It became murderous because Victor abandoned it. "I was benevolent and good," the creature says, "misery made me a fiend."

Besides, the "maybe don't build it" advice has the same problem as "think before you act." It assumes you can know in advance what you're building. You often can't. What you can control is how you respond to what you've built. That moment—the morning after success—is where you actually have choices.

## CLOSE_WITH_GIFT

The gift of the Frankenstein story isn't a warning against ambition. It's a case study in what happens when you value what you can make over what you've made. Victor's tragedy isn't that he created something beyond his control. It's that he never tried to build a relationship with it.

Whatever you're building—a company, a technology, a piece of writing—remember that success creates a new thing in the world that will make demands on you. Your job isn't done when you bring it to life. That's when your job starts.
